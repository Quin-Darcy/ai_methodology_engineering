# Authoritative Sources for a Claude-Based Philosophical Literature-Review Project: A Methodological Reference Map

## TL;DR

The existing baseline is largely sound and should be retained, but it has three correctable weaknesses: (a) for **research methodology in philosophy itself**, the strongest single anchor is missing — Cappelen, Gendler & Hawthorne's *Oxford Handbook of Philosophical Methodology* (2016), with Daly's *Palgrave Handbook* (2015) and Williamson's *Doing Philosophy* (2018) as complements rather than replacements; (b) the principle-of-charity strand needs to be re-grounded in primary Davidson and Quine texts plus the recent Stevens (2021) defense and the *Topoi* (2025) special issue, because Dennett's "Rapoport's Rules" is a popular reformulation rather than the canonical methodological norm; (c) for adapting systematic/scoping methodology to philosophy, the most directly relevant primary sources are Sofaer & Strech (2012) "The Need for Systematic Reviews of Reasons" and Polonioli (2019) "A Plea for Minimally Biased Naturalistic Philosophy" — these should be added because Arksey/O'Malley, Levac et al., Tricco et al., Thomas/Harden, and Noblit/Hare are health/social-science sources whose transfer to philosophy is contested and not direct.

The research request also exposes a real gap: there is **no canonical, dedicated methodological treatment of "the philosophical literature review" as a genre**. Most of what the user wants must be assembled by analogy from (i) generalist philosophical-writing guides, (ii) meta-philosophy and history-of-philosophy methodology, and (iii) systematic-review methodology imported from neighboring fields — and the procedure document should say so explicitly.

The recommended Tier 1 core is tight: **eight indispensable items**, organized below.

## Findings by Research Area

### Area 1 — Philosophical Question Formulation and Refinement

This is the area with the **thinnest dedicated literature**. Philosophy has no equivalent of the social sciences' "research question" methodology textbook. The best resources are pedagogical writing guides plus a handful of reflective monographs.

**Pryor, James. "Guidelines on Writing a Philosophy Paper." jimpryor.net (rev. 2006; widely mirrored at MIT, Brandeis, UWO, U of Toronto-Scarborough).**
- *Evidence level:* INFLUENTIAL (de facto canonical pedagogically; not peer-reviewed).
- *Rationale:* The most widely cited online guide for narrowing a philosophical thesis. Pryor's core directive — "A good philosophy paper is modest and makes a small point; but it makes that point clearly and straightforwardly, and it offers good reasons in support of it" — operationalizes "well-formed question" as "thesis narrow enough to be defended in the available space, with the dialectical position to its opponents made explicit." Multiple top philosophy departments link to it as their default writing guide.
- *Status:* Confirms baseline. Treat as influential pedagogical reference, not as scholarly authority.

**Martinich, A. P. *Philosophical Writing: An Introduction*. 5th edition. Hoboken, NJ: Wiley-Blackwell, 2024 (ISBN 9781394193394). Earlier editions: 1st 1989 (Prentice-Hall); 2nd 1996; 3rd 2005; 4th 2015 (Wiley-Blackwell).**
- *Evidence level:* INFLUENTIAL.
- *Rationale:* The standard textbook treatment, organized around the practical sequence of moving from topic to thesis to defended argument. The 4th edition added a chapter on "how to read a philosophical essay" and sections on rhetoric in philosophical writing; the 5th adds chapters on belief networks and properties of sound interpretations. Where Pryor is a single-essay handout, Martinich is a sustained book-length treatment of the same skills.
- *Status:* Confirms baseline. Use the most recent edition the user can obtain; flag edition-specific chapter numbering when quoting.

**Williamson, Timothy. *Doing Philosophy: From Common Curiosity to Logical Reasoning*. Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2018 (ISBN 9780198822516); reissued in paperback as *Philosophical Method: A Very Short Introduction*, OUP 2020 (ISBN 9780198810001).**
- *Evidence level:* INFLUENTIAL.
- *Rationale:* The most accessible recent statement, by a major figure, of the methodological self-understanding of mainstream analytic philosophy: that philosophy "begins in common sense curiosity" and proceeds through dispute, conceptual clarification, thought experiments, deduction, and theory-comparison. Useful for Area 1 because Williamson treats *what counts as a tractable philosophical question* implicitly throughout. Note that the *VSI* (2020) is the same text as *Doing Philosophy* (2018), repackaged — the user should treat them as one work.
- *Status:* Confirms baseline; flag the dual-publication issue.

**Daly, Christopher (ed.). *The Palgrave Handbook of Philosophical Methods*. Basingstoke: Palgrave Macmillan, 2015 (ISBN 9781137344540; 26 chapters, ~698 pp.).** *and* **Daly, Christopher. *An Introduction to Philosophical Methods*. Peterborough, ON: Broadview Press, 2010 (ISBN 9781551119342; Broadview Guides to Philosophy).**
- *Evidence level:* SUPPLEMENTARY (Handbook); INFLUENTIAL (textbook).
- *Rationale:* The 2010 monograph is the first sustained survey of the methods analytic philosophers actually use (common sense, conceptual analysis, thought experiments, simplicity, explanation, naturalism). The 2015 Handbook collects 26 commissioned chapters by leading figures and is the most recent serious anthology. Both supplement Williamson and Martinich by treating *which methods are appropriate for which kinds of question* — the missing middle term between "writing well" and "doing philosophy."
- *Status:* SUPPLEMENT to baseline. The textbook is dispensable if the Handbook is on hand, but is a more student-friendly entry point.

> **Honest gap:** No mainstream source provides a stepwise *operational* method for "turning a vague topic into a researchable philosophical question" comparable to what social-science research-methods textbooks provide. Michel Meyer's *Of Problematology* (Chicago, 1995) is the closest dedicated philosophical theory of question-formation, but it is a continental-tradition theoretical work, not a practical guide, and has limited uptake in mainstream Anglophone methodology. The procedure document should not pretend this gap doesn't exist.

### Area 2 — The Source Ecology of Philosophy

This area is well-served by reference works themselves and by a small body of bibliometric work; there is no single dedicated treatise.

**Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (SEP). Edward N. Zalta and Uri Nodelman, eds. https://plato.stanford.edu/. The Metaphysics Research Lab, Stanford University.**
- *Evidence level:* CANONICAL (as a discipline reference work).
- *Rationale:* SEP is the de facto authoritative reference work for mainstream Anglophone philosophy. Each entry is commissioned, peer-reviewed by subject editors, and revised over time; archived "fixed editions" are issued every three months (21 September, December, March, June) so that quoted passages remain stable and citable. The SEP itself documents this in its citation guide. Schwitzgebel's analyses of SEP citation patterns (most recently the Schwitzgebel & Jackson "253 Most-Cited Works" data, *Daily Nous*, March 2025) demonstrate that SEP citation rates are widely treated as the best available proxy for influence in mainstream Anglophone philosophy.
- *Status:* Add as TIER 1. Procedurally: any quotation should cite the archived edition, not the live page.

**Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy (IEP). https://iep.utm.edu/. James Fieser and Bradley Dowden, eds.**
- *Evidence level:* INFLUENTIAL (lower threshold than SEP).
- *Rationale:* Peer-reviewed, broader topical coverage in some areas (especially continental and history-of-philosophy topics where SEP coverage is uneven), but less consistently authored by the leading specialists. Useful as a second opinion or for topics where SEP is missing or thin.
- *Status:* SUPPLEMENT to baseline. Treat as second-tier to SEP.

**Routledge Encyclopedia of Philosophy (REP). https://www.rep.routledge.com/. Edward Craig, gen. ed.**
- *Evidence level:* INFLUENTIAL.
- *Rationale:* The major commercial reference encyclopedia, more catholic in coverage than SEP (better continental, non-Western, and historical coverage in many areas). Subscription-gated, which limits its utility for an open-access-leaning project but makes it valuable when accessible. Its entry on "Charity, principle of" is a good example of the kind of compact, expert overview SEP sometimes lacks.
- *Status:* SUPPLEMENT to baseline.

**Cullars, John M. "Citation characteristics of English-language monographs in philosophy." *Library & Information Science Research* 20.1 (1998): 41–68.**
- *Evidence level:* SUPPLEMENTARY (older but uniquely relevant).
- *Rationale:* The standard citation study showing that "while many contemporary philosophers consider their discipline more related to the sciences than to the humanities, their citation patterns are typically humanistic, with the bulk of citations to books rather than journal articles and the citation of much material older than 20 years." This is the empirical ground for the procedure document's claim that philosophy's source ecology privileges monographs and tolerates much older sources — a sharp contrast with biomedical/STEM norms where currency dominates.
- *Status:* SUPPLEMENT (not in baseline). Add to ground claims about discipline-specific bibliographic norms.

**de Bruin, Boudewijn. "Ranking philosophy journals: a meta-ranking and a new survey ranking." *Synthese* 202.4 (2023): article 130. DOI 10.1007/s11229-023-04342-9.** *and* **Leiter, Brian. "Best 'general' philosophy journals" surveys. https://leiterreports.com/ (most recent 2022 cycle).**
- *Evidence level:* SUPPLEMENTARY.
- *Rationale:* de Bruin's *Synthese* paper is the only peer-reviewed treatment of philosophy journal rankings; it constructs a meta-ranking from the Leiter surveys, Scopus/Scimago, Google Scholar, and Web of Science, and complements this with its own 2022 survey of 351 respondents. Leiter's pairwise-comparison surveys are the de facto reputational ranking the discipline uses. Together they let the procedure document take a defensible position on journal prestige (Philosophical Review, Noûs, Philosophy & Phenomenological Research, Mind, Journal of Philosophy as the consensus top tier) while acknowledging — as de Bruin does — that any single ranking is "vibes-based" and the meta-ranking is the more methodologically defensible artifact.
- *Status:* SUPPLEMENT. Use de Bruin as the citable scholarly source; Leiter as the supporting community-recognition source.

> **Caveat:** The "Leiter rankings" the user named in the prompt are technically Leiter's *general philosophy journal* rankings (which heavily privilege epistemology/metaphysics) and his department rankings (the *Philosophical Gourmet Report*). These are widely used but contested; commenters in the de Bruin discussion correctly note that the "general" rankings systematically underweight ethics, history of philosophy, philosophy of race, feminist philosophy, and aesthetics. The procedure document should treat the rankings as indicative, not authoritative.

### Area 3 — Tradition-Appropriate Research Methods

This area is best served by canonical historiographical statements (Skinner, Rorty, Bevir) and by recent meta-philosophical surveys of the analytic/continental divide.

**Skinner, Quentin. *Visions of Politics, Volume 1: Regarding Method*. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 2002 (ISBN 9780521589260).** *and* **Skinner, Quentin. "Meaning and Understanding in the History of Ideas." *History and Theory* 8.1 (1969): 3–53. JSTOR 2504188.**
- *Evidence level:* CANONICAL.
- *Rationale:* Skinner's 1969 essay is, by general consensus among intellectual historians, the founding statement of the contextualist (Cambridge School) approach: that recovering authorial intention requires reconstructing the linguistic-conventional context, and that ahistorical "rational reconstruction" produces "mythology, not history." *Regarding Method* (2002) collects Skinner's revised and extended methodological essays. The 2025 Liverpool/British Academy volume *Meaning and Understanding in the History of Ideas and Beyond* (ed. A. Blau) marks the 50th-anniversary reassessment and is useful for the current state of the debate.
- *Status:* Confirms baseline.

**Rorty, Richard. "The Historiography of Philosophy: Four Genres." Originally in Rorty, Schneewind, and Skinner, eds., *Philosophy in History*, Cambridge UP, 1984; reprinted as Chapter 13 of Rorty, *Truth and Progress: Philosophical Papers, Volume 3*, Cambridge UP, 1998 (DOI 10.1017/CBO9780511625404.014).**
- *Evidence level:* CANONICAL.
- *Rationale:* Rorty's four-genre taxonomy — rational reconstruction, historical reconstruction, *Geistesgeschichte*, and doxography — is the standard map of how analytic philosophers approach the history of their field. Rorty's central argument is that the apparent dilemma between treating past philosophers as contemporary interlocutors versus historicizing them is false, because both kinds of work are needed and serve different purposes. This is *the* counterpoint to Skinner: where Skinner prohibits ahistorical refutation, Rorty defends it (alongside historical reconstruction) as legitimate.
- *Status:* Confirms baseline.

> **Surface this disagreement explicitly:** Skinner's contextualism and Rorty's pluralism are genuinely incompatible at their strongest formulations — Skinner holds that "philosophers of different times do not speak directly to us" and that there are "no perennial problems," while Rorty defends rational reconstruction as a legitimate genre that necessarily lets contemporary concerns frame the past. The procedure document should not paper this over.

**Bevir, Mark. *The Logic of the History of Ideas*. Cambridge: Cambridge University Press, 1999 (ISBN 9780521640343; paperback 0521016843).**
- *Evidence level:* INFLUENTIAL.
- *Rationale:* The most rigorous post-Skinnerian philosophical analysis of the methodology of the history of ideas. Bevir explicitly distances himself from Skinner's claim that a *method* can guarantee objectivity: "no method can constitute a form of justification. A method can perform a useful heuristic role, but it cannot give us a logical guarantee of the objectivity of an understanding of a work." This makes Bevir the natural third reference point, alongside Skinner and Rorty, when the procedure document needs to justify its interpretive procedures without overclaiming.
- *Status:* SUPPLEMENT (not in baseline). Recommended addition.

**Cappelen, Herman, Tamar Szabó Gendler, and John Hawthorne, eds. *The Oxford Handbook of Philosophical Methodology*. Oxford: Oxford University Press, 2016 (ISBN 9780199668779; ~752 pp., 36 chapters by 38 authors). DOI 10.1093/oxfordhb/9780199668779.001.0001.**
- *Evidence level:* CANONICAL (for current methodological debate in mainstream philosophy).
- *Rationale:* The most comprehensive available reference on philosophical methodology. Part I covers traditions and approaches (logical empiricism, phenomenology, ordinary-language philosophy, etc.); Part II covers methodological topics (intuitions, conceptual analysis, transcendental arguments); Part III covers philosophy's interfaces with neighboring fields. Particularly relevant for Area 3: Fred Beiser's "History of Ideas: A Defense" (Ch. 26), Christian List & Laura Valentini's "The Methodology of Political Theory" (Ch. 27), and the methodological tradition chapters in Part I. NDPR's review (Matthew Haug, 2017) calls it "outstanding and timely" and the best available place for a sophisticated introduction to current debates.
- *Status:* SUPPLEMENT (not in baseline). **This is the largest single addition I recommend.** It is missing from the baseline and partially supersedes parts of Williamson's role.

**Glendinning, Simon. *The Idea of Continental Philosophy: A Philosophical Chronicle*. Edinburgh: Edinburgh University Press, 2006 (DOI 10.3366/edinburgh/9780748624706.001.0001).** *and* **Reynolds, Jack, and James Chase. *Analytic versus Continental: Arguments on the Methods and Value of Philosophy*. Stocksfield: Acumen, 2011.**
- *Evidence level:* INFLUENTIAL.
- *Rationale:* Glendinning argues that the term "continental philosophy" originated as a pejorative label assigned by analytic philosophers and that the divide is significantly an artifact of mid-20th-century institutional politics; Reynolds & Chase give the most balanced book-length comparative treatment of the methodological commitments of each tradition. Together they let the procedure document treat the analytic/continental distinction without naturalizing it, while still using it as a working heuristic for tradition-appropriate research methods.
- *Status:* SUPPLEMENT (not in baseline).

**Zahavi, Dan. *Phenomenology: The Basics*. 2nd edition. London: Routledge, 2023 (ISBN 9781032396378).**
- *Evidence level:* SUPPLEMENTARY.
- *Rationale:* The clearest accessible introduction to phenomenological method (as distinct from phenomenology as a body of doctrine), by the leading contemporary scholar of Husserl. Useful when the project takes on continental-tradition questions, where Williamson, Daly, and Cappelen et al. are essentially silent.
- *Status:* SUPPLEMENT (not in baseline). Add only if continental-tradition questions are common in the project's pipeline.

### Area 4 — Charitable Reading and the Principle of Charity

The user's prompt anticipates correctly that this area has "more passing mention than dedicated treatment" — that judgment is right, and the recommended sources reflect it.

**Davidson, Donald. "Radical Interpretation." *Dialectica* 27 (1973): 313–328; reprinted in Davidson, *Inquiries into Truth and Interpretation*, Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1984 (and 2nd ed., 2001).** *and* **Davidson, Donald. "On the Very Idea of a Conceptual Scheme." *Proceedings and Addresses of the American Philosophical Association* 47 (1973–74): 5–20; reprinted in *Inquiries* (1984).**
- *Evidence level:* CANONICAL.
- *Rationale:* The locus classicus. Davidson's formulation — "charity is forced on us; whether we like it or not, if we want to understand others, we must count them right in most matters" (1974, in *Inquiries* 1984: 197) — establishes charity not as a moral norm but as a constitutive condition of interpretation. The Stanford Encyclopedia entry on Davidson (Malpas) and the *Companion to Donald Davidson* chapter "Radical Interpretation and the Principle of Charity" (Pagin 2013, DOI 10.1002/9781118328408.ch13) are the standard secondary entry points.
- *Status:* SUPPLEMENT (named in baseline only generically). Add as Tier 1 primary source.

**Quine, W. V. O. *Word and Object*. Cambridge, MA: MIT Press, 1960 (esp. §13 on radical translation), particularly the formulation: "one's interlocutor's silliness, beyond a certain point, is less likely than bad translation" (1960: 59).**
- *Evidence level:* CANONICAL.
- *Rationale:* The original modern formulation that Davidson built on. Together Quine and Davidson define the analytic-philosophical understanding of the principle.
- *Status:* SUPPLEMENT (named in baseline only generically). Add as Tier 1 primary source.

**Stevens, Katharina. "Charity for Moral Reasons? — A Defense of the Principle of Charity in Argumentation." *Argumentation and Advocacy* 57.2 (2021): 67–84. DOI 10.1080/10511431.2021.1897327.** *and* **Stevens, Katharina. "Introduction to the Special Issue on the Principle of Charity in Argumentation." *Topoi* 44 (2025): 1355–1358. DOI 10.1007/s11245-025-10331-z.**
- *Evidence level:* INFLUENTIAL (recent and methodologically focused).
- *Rationale:* The most carefully argued recent defense of charity as a methodological-cum-moral norm in argumentation theory specifically (rather than philosophy of language). Stevens defends "a pro tanto moral duty to be charitable in argument" and argues that the amount of effort required varies with dialogue type. The 2025 *Topoi* special issue she edits is the current state of the literature on the topic. The Springer 2024 paper "Charity Principles in Philosophical Argumentation" (DOI 10.1007/s10503-024-09648-7) is a useful taxonomic companion.
- *Status:* SUPPLEMENT (not in baseline). Strongly recommended addition because it gives the user a defensible argumentation-theoretic grounding for the procedure document's "charitable reconstruction" requirement.

**Dennett, Daniel C. *Intuition Pumps and Other Tools for Thinking*. New York: W. W. Norton, 2013, Ch. 3 ("Rapoport's Rules"), pp. 31–35.**
- *Evidence level:* SUPPLEMENTARY.
- *Rationale:* Dennett's four-step formulation — re-express the target's position so well they say "Thanks, I wish I'd thought of putting it that way," then list points of agreement, then list things learned, *only then* criticize — is the popularization that most people now mean by "steelmanning." Note Dennett himself credits the rules to Anatol Rapoport (and acknowledges that his formulation is "from memory of correspondence … now apparently lost"), so this is a popularized norm, not a primary methodological source.
- *Status:* Add as supplementary reference (not Tier 1) — clearly identify it as a popularization rather than the principle's canonical statement, and pair it with Davidson/Quine in the procedure document.

> **Honest gap (the user predicted this correctly):** There is no single dedicated, peer-reviewed treatise on "the principle of charity as a methodological norm for literature reviews" or for history-of-philosophy interpretation. The literature is fragmented across philosophy of language (Davidson/Quine), informal logic and argumentation theory (Walton, Govier, Stevens, the 2024 Springer *Argumentation* taxonomy), popularization (Dennett, "steelmanning" online), and metaphilosophical historiography (Adamson's blog "rules" for history of philosophy, and the Schwitzgebel/Adamson exchange "Against Charity in the History of Philosophy"). The procedure document should explicitly say that "the principle of charity" is being deployed as a methodological norm assembled from these multiple strands, not as a single canonical doctrine. Note also the recent feminist critique (in the *Grokipedia* article and in Stevens 2021) that uneven charity demands can themselves be epistemically unjust — relevant for any project that may engage marginalized authors.

### Area 5 — Philosophical Literature Review vs. Empirical Systematic Review

This is structurally the most important area for the project, and the area where the baseline is most exposed: Arksey/O'Malley, Levac et al., Tricco et al., Thomas/Harden, Noblit/Hare, and Booth/Sutton/Papaioannou are all health-and-social-science methodology sources whose transfer to philosophy is genuinely contested.

**Arksey, Hilary, and Lisa O'Malley. "Scoping studies: towards a methodological framework." *International Journal of Social Research Methodology* 8.1 (2005): 19–32. DOI 10.1080/1364557032000119616.**
- *Evidence level:* CANONICAL (for the scoping-review methodology being adapted).
- *Rationale:* The originating six-stage framework: identifying the research question, identifying relevant studies, study selection, charting the data, collating/summarizing/reporting, and the optional consultation stage. This is the foundational citation for any scoping-style review in any field.
- *Status:* Confirms baseline.

**Levac, Danielle, Heather Colquhoun, and Kelly K. O'Brien. "Scoping studies: advancing the methodology." *Implementation Science* 5 (2010): 69. DOI 10.1186/1748-5908-5-69.**
- *Evidence level:* CANONICAL (refinement of Arksey/O'Malley).
- *Rationale:* Tightens the data-analysis stage and the consultation stage, which Arksey and O'Malley left under-specified. The two papers are read together in current practice.
- *Status:* Confirms baseline.

**Tricco, Andrea C., Erin Lillie, Wasifa Zarin, et al. "PRISMA Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR): Checklist and Explanation." *Annals of Internal Medicine* 169.7 (2018): 467–473. DOI 10.7326/M18-0850.**
- *Evidence level:* CANONICAL (for reporting standards).
- *Rationale:* The 20-item (+2 optional) reporting checklist that has become the standard for scoping reviews. It is what the user should imitate *structurally* in the procedure document, while honestly noting that several items (e.g., risk-of-bias assessment, registered protocols) translate poorly into philosophy.
- *Status:* Confirms baseline.

**Booth, Andrew, Anthea Sutton, and Diana Papaioannou. *Systematic Approaches to a Successful Literature Review*. 2nd edition. London: Sage, 2016 (ISBN 9781473912465). 3rd edition (with Mark Steele added) published 2021 by Sage; 4th edition forthcoming.**
- *Evidence level:* CANONICAL (textbook).
- *Rationale:* The standard graduate textbook on systematic and scoping approaches to literature review across the social sciences. Useful for the procedure document's *general* structure even though it does not specifically address philosophy.
- *Status:* Confirms baseline; ensure the user is using the 2nd or 3rd edition.

**Thomas, James, and Angela Harden. "Methods for the thematic synthesis of qualitative research in systematic reviews." *BMC Medical Research Methodology* 8 (2008): 45. DOI 10.1186/1471-2288-8-45.**
- *Evidence level:* INFLUENTIAL (for synthesis method, but field-displaced).
- *Rationale:* The three-stage thematic-synthesis method (line-by-line coding, descriptive themes, analytical themes). The third stage — generating analytical themes that "go beyond" the primary studies — is structurally analogous to what a philosophical literature review does when it offers a dialectical synthesis of the views surveyed.
- *Status:* Confirms baseline. Use the *structural analogy*, not the empirical method directly; flag the imperfect transfer.

**Noblit, George W., and R. Dwight Hare. *Meta-Ethnography: Synthesizing Qualitative Studies*. Newbury Park, CA: Sage, 1988 (ISBN 9780803930230).**
- *Evidence level:* INFLUENTIAL (for "translation" as synthesis).
- *Rationale:* The seven-stage method of synthesizing across qualitative studies via "reciprocal translation," "refutational translation," and "lines-of-argument synthesis." The translation metaphor is genuinely useful for philosophical synthesis — different schools' vocabularies can be partially translated into one another even when their substantive commitments differ. But the book is now 38 years old and aimed at ethnographic studies, so its translation to philosophy needs to be done carefully and explicitly.
- *Status:* Confirms baseline; use the conceptual apparatus (reciprocal/refutational translation), not the procedural stages directly.

**Sofaer, Neema, and Daniel Strech. "The Need for Systematic Reviews of Reasons." *Bioethics* 26.6 (2012): 315–328. DOI 10.1111/j.1467-8519.2011.01858.x.** *and* **Strech, Daniel, and Neema Sofaer. "How to Write a Systematic Review of Reasons." *Journal of Medical Ethics* 38 (2012): 121–126.**
- *Evidence level:* INFLUENTIAL (and uniquely directly relevant).
- *Rationale:* **The strongest non-baseline addition for Area 5.** Sofaer & Strech explicitly develop a method for the systematic review of *arguments* (rather than empirical findings) in reason-based bioethics — i.e., for systematically identifying and classifying the *reasons* given for and against ethical positions in the literature. This is the closest existing methodological model to what the user's project is trying to do for philosophy generally. Multiple subsequent reviews have applied the framework (e.g., the 2025 *Bioethics* paper on somatic gene therapy; the 2020 paper on genomic data sharing in pediatrics).
- *Status:* SUPPLEMENT (not in baseline). **Strongly recommended addition.**

**Polonioli, Andrea. "A Plea for Minimally Biased Naturalistic Philosophy." *Synthese* 196.9 (2019): 3841–3867 (online 30 Nov 2017). DOI 10.1007/s11229-017-1628-0.**
- *Evidence level:* INFLUENTIAL (and uniquely directly relevant).
- *Rationale:* Polonioli argues explicitly that philosophers' "literature search and review" is biased in identifiable, systematic ways and that the discipline should adopt systematic-review tools — citing Strech & Sofaer and the broader systematic-review literature — to mitigate this bias. The paper is the only peer-reviewed treatment I have located that argues *directly* for transposing systematic-review methodology into philosophy itself.
- *Status:* SUPPLEMENT (not in baseline). **Strongly recommended addition.** This is exactly the methodological rationale the user's project needs.

**Parsons, J., et al. "In defence of the bioethics scoping review: Largely systematic literature reviewing with broad utility." *Bioethics* 36 (2022): article in vol. 36. DOI 10.1111/bioe.12991.**
- *Evidence level:* SUPPLEMENTARY.
- *Rationale:* A recent practical defense of the scoping-review form for bioethics, arguing it captures "much of the methodological rigour of a systematic review" while permitting interpretive flexibility. Useful for grounding the user's hybrid Phase 1 / Phase 2 design.
- *Status:* SUPPLEMENT (not in baseline).

> **Honest disagreement to surface in the procedure document:** Several authoritative voices in the systematic-review community (Booth's textbook, Munn et al. 2018, Peters et al. 2021) treat the systematic review as an "answer-the-question" tool and the scoping review as a "map-the-field" tool. A philosophical literature review is structurally closer to the *scoping* form (mapping positions and arguments) than to the *systematic* form (aggregating empirical findings). McDougall and others (Sofaer & Strech 2012; McDougall 2014) have noted that several steps of the systematic-review process — risk-of-bias assessment, study quality scoring, statistical synthesis — translate poorly or not at all into a dialectical, argument-based field. The procedure document should explicitly take the position that it is borrowing the *transparency and reproducibility ethos* of systematic reviewing but adopting a scoping-review structure plus Sofaer/Strech-style review-of-reasons synthesis.

## Tiered Acquisition List

### Tier 1: acquire first (the indispensable core, ~8 items)

1. **Cappelen, Gendler & Hawthorne, eds., *The Oxford Handbook of Philosophical Methodology* (OUP 2016).** The single largest gap in the existing baseline.
2. **Williamson, *Doing Philosophy* (OUP 2018) / *Philosophical Method: A VSI* (OUP 2020).** Same text, two packagings.
3. **Skinner, *Visions of Politics, Vol. 1: Regarding Method* (CUP 2002).** Contextualism, canonical.
4. **Rorty, "The Historiography of Philosophy: Four Genres" in *Truth and Progress* (CUP 1998).** Pluralist counterpoint to Skinner.
5. **Davidson, *Inquiries into Truth and Interpretation* (Clarendon 1984; 2nd ed. 2001), esp. "Radical Interpretation."** Primary source for the principle of charity.
6. **Booth, Sutton & Papaioannou, *Systematic Approaches to a Successful Literature Review*, 2nd or 3rd ed. (Sage 2016/2021).** Operational backbone.
7. **Arksey & O'Malley (2005) + Tricco et al. PRISMA-ScR (2018).** Treat as a unit; the actual structural model.
8. **Sofaer & Strech, "The Need for Systematic Reviews of Reasons," *Bioethics* (2012)** + **Polonioli, "A Plea for Minimally Biased Naturalistic Philosophy," *Synthese* (2019).** The two papers that most directly bridge systematic review to philosophy.

### Tier 2: acquire if Tier 1 has gaps

9. Daly, ed., *Palgrave Handbook of Philosophical Methods* (Palgrave 2015) — when Cappelen/Gendler/Hawthorne does not cover an area.
10. Daly, *An Introduction to Philosophical Methods* (Broadview 2010) — student-level entry.
11. Martinich, *Philosophical Writing: An Introduction* (5th ed. Wiley 2024 or 4th ed. 2015) — pragmatic writing guidance.
12. Bevir, *The Logic of the History of Ideas* (CUP 1999) — third anchor in the historiography debate alongside Skinner and Rorty.
13. Levac, Colquhoun & O'Brien (2010) — refinement of Arksey/O'Malley.
14. Thomas & Harden (2008) — for the analytical-themes apparatus.
15. Noblit & Hare, *Meta-Ethnography* (Sage 1988) — for reciprocal/refutational translation.
16. Stevens, "Charity for Moral Reasons?" (*Argumentation and Advocacy* 2021) and *Topoi* 2025 special issue — for the recent argumentation-theoretic ground for charity.

## Caveats and Gaps in the Literature

**1. There is no canonical methodology for "the philosophical literature review" as a genre.** Everything in Area 5 is an analogy from neighboring fields. The most direct non-analogy work — Sofaer & Strech 2012, Polonioli 2019, Parsons 2022 — is recent, narrow (largely bioethics), and not yet consolidated into a textbook. The procedure document should say this honestly rather than write as if it were operationalizing an established philosophical-literature-review method.

**2. The principle of charity is more invoked than analyzed as a methodological norm.** As the user correctly anticipated, the canonical Davidson and Quine formulations are about radical interpretation in the philosophy of language, not about how to conduct a literature review or read past philosophers charitably. The argumentation-theory literature (Stevens, the 2024 Springer *Argumentation* paper, the 2025 *Topoi* issue) is the most methodologically explicit but is recent and not yet canonical. "Steelmanning" and Dennett's "Rapoport's Rules" are popularizations that should not be cited as primary methodological sources. There is also a live critique — feminist and from the history-of-philosophy side (Schwitzgebel; Lewinski's "paradox of charity" 2012) — that universal charity demands can themselves produce epistemic injustice and historical anachronism. The procedure document should treat charity as a defeasible norm rather than an absolute one.

**3. Skinner vs. Rorty is a real, unresolved disagreement.** Skinner's contextualism prohibits the very practice (rational reconstruction across centuries) that Rorty defends as a legitimate genre. Any philosophical literature review that engages historical figures has to take a position on this — the procedure document's most defensible move is Rorty's pluralism (treat the genres as complementary), but Skinner's contextualist warnings about anachronism should be operationalized as a check against rational-reconstruction excesses. The 2025 Liverpool/British Academy volume is the best current state-of-the-art assessment of this debate.

**4. The "source ecology" question is poorly served by the literature.** Cullars (1998) is the most cited citation study of philosophy and is now 28 years old. Schwitzgebel's SEP-citation analyses are continuing but live on a blog; de Bruin (2023) is the only peer-reviewed journal-ranking study. The procedure document is, in part, doing original methodological work here that the published literature has not done — and should be epistemically honest that the rankings and rules of thumb it relies on (SEP > IEP, top-five generalist journals = Phil Review/Noûs/PPR/Mind/JPhil, monographs heavily weighted, older citations tolerated) are *defensible community consensus* rather than *empirically validated bibliometric findings*.

**5. The analytic/continental divide is contested even at the level of whether it is a genuine methodological distinction.** Glendinning treats it as substantially an artifact of mid-20th-century institutional politics; Williamson (and most current mainstream practice) effectively assimilates continental work to analytic standards when engaging with it; some continental philosophers reject the category. The procedure document should treat the divide as a working heuristic, not as a deep methodological taxonomy.

**6. Confidence calibration on the recommendations themselves.** I have read or directly verified passages from the SEP, Davidson's principle-of-charity formulation (via the SEP "Davidson" entry and Pagin 2013), Skinner's 1969 essay (via abstracts and the Liverpool 2025 volume), Rorty's "Four Genres" essay (via Cambridge Core and PhilPapers), the Arksey/O'Malley/Levac/Tricco/Thomas-Harden/Noblit-Hare citations (multiple direct primary-source confirmations), Polonioli (2019, full text via PMC), and Sofaer & Strech (2012, abstract and PhilPapers entry). I am recommending Williamson, Cappelen/Gendler/Hawthorne, Daly, Martinich, Booth/Sutton/Papaioannou, Bevir, Glendinning, Zahavi, Stevens (2021), de Bruin (2023), and Cullars (1998) on the basis of publisher metadata, NDPR or analogous reviews, and reputable secondary citations rather than direct reading of the full texts. Pryor I have read in primary form. Dennett's "Rapoport's Rules" I have verified the wording of via the PDF of *Intuition Pumps*. Edition and pagination details for Martinich (the book has gone through five editions, 1989–2024, with substantial structural changes) and Booth/Sutton/Papaioannou (now in a 3rd edition) should be checked at the point of acquisition rather than trusted from this report.
