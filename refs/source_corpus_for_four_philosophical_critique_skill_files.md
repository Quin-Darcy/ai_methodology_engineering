# Source Corpus for Four Philosophical-Critique Skill Files

## TL;DR

- **All four user-proposed canonical baselines validate as best-in-class primary sources** — keep van Eemeren & Grootendorst (2004), Walton/Reed/Macagno (2008), Gadamer (1960/2004), Ricoeur (1981), Skinner (2002), and Dennett (2013) as canonical. The only modification is to add Skinner's 1969 essay "Meaning and Understanding in the History of Ideas" as a co-canonical anchor since Mode 3's most-cited methodological text is reprinted *as Chapter 4 of Visions of Politics Vol. 1*, but is more frequently cited in its standalone form.
- **The strongest implementation sources for dialogical-move extraction are**: van Eemeren/Grootendorst/Snoeck Henkemans, *Argumentation: Analysis, Evaluation, Presentation* (2002) and Walton's *Methods of Argumentation* (2013) for Mode 1; Palmer (1969) and Weinsheimer (1985) for Mode 2; Skinner's *Foundations* (1978) and *Liberty Before Liberalism* (1998) for Mode 3; Dennett's *Consciousness Explained* (1991) and *Elbow Room* (1984) for Mode 4. These contain explicit step-by-step worked examples on concrete texts/arguments.
- **The single highest-priority acquisition per mode** (skill-authoring critical path) is: Mode 1 — *Argumentation: Analysis, Evaluation, Presentation* (most concrete dialogical moves); Mode 2 — Palmer's *Hermeneutics* + the Part II of *Truth and Method*; Mode 3 — Skinner's *Visions of Politics* Vol. 1 + Tully's *Meaning and Context*; Mode 4 — *Intuition Pumps* (2013) + Cappelen's *Philosophy without Intuitions* (2012). One caveat applies across all four modes: **textbook-style step-by-step worked examples are scarcer for Mode 2 (hermeneutics) and Mode 3 (Skinnerian reconstruction) than for Modes 1 and 4**, because both methods are presented in their primary literature as reflective practice rather than as algorithmic procedures. For these two modes, "implementation" in the user's sense is best satisfied by reading the canonical historian/hermeneuticist's *substantive applied work* alongside a chapter-level expository commentary.

## MODE 1 — PRAGMA-DIALECTICAL CRITIQUE

### Canonical primary sources — VALIDATED

**Frans H. van Eemeren & Rob Grootendorst, *A Systematic Theory of Argumentation: The Pragma-Dialectical Approach*, Cambridge University Press, 2004 (ISBN 0-521-83075-3 hardback / 0-521-53772-X paperback).**

- This is the consolidated mature statement of the framework, integrating the four-stage model, ten rules, and fallacy-as-rule-violation account in one volume. Use Chapter 3 (model of critical discussion), Chapter 6 (rules for critical discussion — the canonical ten-rule list), and Chapter 7 (fallacies). For a Claude Code skill that emits dialogical critiques, the rule list and fallacy-mapping in Chapters 6–7 give the operational core.
- **Recommended companion update**: van Eemeren, *Argumentation Theory: A Pragma-Dialectical Perspective* (Springer, 2018), which adds strategic maneuvering and updates the framework. Useful but not necessary for a v1 skill.

**Douglas Walton, Christopher Reed & Fabrizio Macagno, *Argumentation Schemes*, Cambridge University Press, 2008 (ISBN 9780521723749).**

- The reference catalog of ~60 schemes, each with a stated form (premises/conclusion) and a paired set of critical questions. Chapters 3 (basic schemes) and 9 (schemes with their full critical-question sets) are the operative chapters. For a skill that needs to issue concrete dialogical challenges, the critical-question lists are the most directly extractable material in any Mode-1 source.

### Implementation / textbook treatments (ranked)

**1. Frans H. van Eemeren, Rob Grootendorst & A. Francisca Snoeck Henkemans, *Argumentation: Analysis, Evaluation, Presentation*, Lawrence Erlbaum / Routledge, 2002 (ISBN 0-8058-3952-6).**

- This is the textbook companion to the *Systematic Theory*, written by the same Amsterdam group, and the only English-language pragma-dialectical textbook by the originators. It walks through identifying differences of opinion, reconstructing argumentative discourse, mapping multiple/coordinative/subordinative argumentation, identifying argument schemes, and applying the rules — all on extended worked examples drawn from real discourse and texts (newspaper editorials, policy debates, op-eds, Calvin and Hobbes strips). End-of-chapter exercises with answers. **This is the single highest-value implementation source for Mode 1.**

**2. Douglas Walton, *Methods of Argumentation*, Cambridge University Press, 2013 (ISBN 9781107677333).**

- Each chapter applies a scheme/cluster of schemes and its critical questions to a leading argumentation problem (legal arguments, political arguments, ad hominem, expert opinion, slippery slope, practical reasoning) on real instances. Walton's treatment is more move-by-move than the *Argumentation Schemes* reference book, and explicitly aims to "be used as tools to analyze arguments." Use Chapters 1–4 for the core methodology demonstrated on live examples.

**3. Douglas Walton, *Fundamentals of Critical Argumentation*, Cambridge University Press, 2006 (ISBN 9780521530200).**

- Beginner-level textbook; introduces the most common scheme types, walks through evaluation by critical questioning on dialogue examples (legal, political, ethical), with exercises. Less dense than #2 but the dialogue-form examples are useful templates for a critic-mode skill that must produce dialogical moves.

### Critique / limits sources (ranked)

**1. Ralph H. Johnson, *Manifest Rationality: A Pragmatic Theory of Argument*, Lawrence Erlbaum, 2000 (ISBN 0-8058-2174-0).**

- Johnson's principal alternative — the "dialectical tier" view — is developed in direct contrast with pragma-dialectics. Chapters 5–7 articulate the charge that pragma-dialectics is over-proceduralized and treats argument too narrowly as resolution-of-difference rather than as rational persuasion. The single most-cited book-length critique from within informal logic.

**2. Christopher W. Tindale, *Acts of Arguing: A Rhetorical Model of Argument*, SUNY Press, 1999 (ISBN 9780791443880).**

- Chapter 2 ("Argumentation as Dialectical") is a sustained critique of the pragma-dialectical procedural model, charging it with neglect of audience and rhetorical context; section 2.5 ("A Critical Evaluation") is the operative passage. The case studies (Shell/Saro-Wiwa; Holocaust-denial trial) show what pragma-dialectical analysis allegedly cannot capture.

**3. C. L. Hamblin, *Fallacies*, Methuen, 1970 (reprinted Vale Press, 2004; ISBN 0-916475-24-7).**

- The foundational critique whose challenge pragma-dialectics was designed to answer. Chapters 1 ("The Standard Treatment") and 8 ("Formal Dialectic") supply the framework against which pragma-dialectics' adequacy is still measured. Older but indispensable; pragma-dialectics's normative ambition cannot be discussed without it.

**Note on coverage**: David Zarefsky, "The Ten Rules of Pragma-Dialectics and Validity in Argumentation," in van Rees & Houtlosser (eds.), *Considering Pragma-Dialectics* (Erlbaum, 2006), is a useful chapter-length internal critique specifically of the rule list, if a fourth source is wanted.

### What each source contributes that the others do not

- *Systematic Theory* gives the consolidated rules; *Argumentation Schemes* gives the scheme-and-critical-question pairs (different unit of analysis).
- *Argumentation: Analysis, Evaluation, Presentation* is the only full textbook walking through reconstruction step-by-step; *Methods of Argumentation* is scheme-focused and more advanced.
- Johnson attacks the resolution-of-difference framing; Tindale attacks the procedural neglect of rhetoric/audience; Hamblin supplies the historical/dialectical baseline against which both pragma-dialectics and its critics are measured.

## MODE 2 — HERMENEUTIC CLOSE-READING

### Canonical primary sources — VALIDATED

**Hans-Georg Gadamer, *Truth and Method*, 2nd revised English edition, trans. Joel Weinsheimer & Donald G. Marshall, Continuum/Bloomsbury, 2004 (German original 1960; ISBN 9780826476975).**

- Part II ("The extension of the question of truth to understanding in the human sciences"), especially II.2 ("Elements of a theory of hermeneutic experience" — covering the rehabilitation of prejudice, the historicity of understanding, the hermeneutic circle, the fusion of horizons, and the dialogue of question and answer) is the operational heart for a close-reading skill. Pages roughly 265–379 in the standard 2004 edition.

**Paul Ricoeur, *Hermeneutics and the Human Sciences: Essays on Language, Action and Interpretation*, ed. and trans. John B. Thompson, Cambridge University Press, 1981 (reprinted with new preface by Charles Taylor, 2016; ISBN 9781316507421).**

- Specifically Chapter 4 ("The hermeneutical function of distanciation"), Chapter 5 ("What is a text? Explanation and understanding") and Chapter 8 ("The model of the text: meaningful action considered as a text"). These three essays articulate the distanciation/appropriation pair, the explanation/understanding dialectic, and text-analogy that supply the most directly extractable dialogical moves for a hermeneutic critic skill.

### Implementation / pedagogical treatments (ranked)

**1. Richard E. Palmer, *Hermeneutics: Interpretation Theory in Schleiermacher, Dilthey, Heidegger, and Gadamer*, Northwestern University Press, 1969 (ISBN 9780810104594).**

- The standard English-language pedagogical introduction. Part I systematically defines the field; Part II treats each major theorist; Part III ("A Hermeneutical Manifesto to American Literary Interpretation") and the closing "Thirty Theses on Interpretation" are the most directly usable material for converting hermeneutics into concrete reading-moves. Still, by 2026, the most assigned Anglophone hermeneutics textbook.

**2. Joel Weinsheimer, *Gadamer's Hermeneutics: A Reading of Truth and Method*, Yale University Press, 1985 (ISBN 9780300033205).**

- A chapter-by-chapter walkthrough of *Truth and Method*. The advantage over Palmer is that it supplies the specific moves Gadamer makes in concrete sections, with elucidation; reviewers note it "translates" the primary text. Use this as the bridge from Gadamer's prose to extractable moves.

**3. Jean Grondin, *Introduction to Philosophical Hermeneutics*, trans. Joel Weinsheimer, Yale University Press, 1994 (German original 1991; ISBN 9780300070897).**

- Broader historical sweep (Philo through Habermas/Derrida); useful for situating Gadamer's moves against alternatives. Foreword by Gadamer himself. Good third source but not as procedurally focused as Palmer or Weinsheimer.

**Honest limitation**: None of these is a pure "step-by-step worked example on a single concrete text" textbook in the sense Mode 1 sources provide for argumentation. Hermeneutics is presented in the literature as reflective practice. The closest to genuine worked-example pedagogy in the canon is Gadamer's own application essays in his *Philosophical Hermeneutics* (1976) — particularly his readings of Celan, Hölderlin, and Plato — which can supplement but not replace the Palmer/Weinsheimer pair.

### Critique / limits sources (ranked)

**1. E. D. Hirsch Jr., *Validity in Interpretation*, Yale University Press, 1967 (ISBN 9780300016925).**

- The classic analytic-tradition counter to Gadamer. Hirsch's distinction between "meaning" (authorial) and "significance" (reader-relative), and the appendix specifically addressing Gadamer ("Gadamer's Theory of Interpretation," pp. 245–264), constitutes the foundational critique of hermeneutic indeterminacy. Indispensable for any pedagogically honest treatment of the limits of hermeneutic close reading.

**2. Diane P. Michelfelder & Richard E. Palmer (eds.), *Dialogue and Deconstruction: The Gadamer-Derrida Encounter*, SUNY Press, 1989 (ISBN 9780791400098).**

- The complete texts of the 1981 Goethe Institute exchange plus follow-up reflections. Derrida's three questions to Gadamer constitute the post-structuralist critique of hermeneutic closure (the charge that hermeneutic dialogue presupposes a "good will" that contains otherness rather than encountering it). Part III's commentaries make the issues operational.

**3. Jürgen Habermas, "The Hermeneutic Claim to Universality" (1970), in Josef Bleicher (ed.), *Contemporary Hermeneutics: Hermeneutics as Method, Philosophy and Critique*, Routledge & Kegan Paul, 1980 (also reprinted in *Knowledge and Human Interests*, Beacon, 1971).**

- The classical Habermas-Gadamer critique: that Gadamerian tradition cannot account for systematically distorted communication and ideology, requiring a critical-theoretic supplement (the depth-hermeneutics of psychoanalysis as model). Demetrius Teigas, *Knowledge and Hermeneutic Understanding: A Study of the Habermas-Gadamer Debate* (Bucknell, 1995) is a useful expository wrapper if needed.

### What each source contributes

- *Truth and Method* Part II is the procedure; Ricoeur's three essays add the explanation/understanding dialectic that Gadamer downplays.
- Palmer is breadth and history; Weinsheimer is a chapter-by-chapter unpacking; Grondin is a corrective historical narrative that questions Gadamer's own self-presentation.
- Hirsch attacks from analytic philosophy of language; Habermas attacks from critical theory; Derrida attacks from deconstruction. Together they cover the three flanks.

## MODE 3 — SKINNERIAN HISTORIOGRAPHICAL RECONSTRUCTION

### Canonical primary sources — VALIDATED with augmentation

**Quentin Skinner, *Visions of Politics, Volume 1: Regarding Method*, Cambridge University Press, 2002 (ISBN 9780521589260).**

- The consolidated, revised statement of the methodology, gathering eight essays revised for republication. Chapter 4 is the revised version of the 1969 "Meaning and Understanding in the History of Ideas," and Chapters 5–8 (on motives/intentions, social context, interpretation) develop the framework. **This volume should be the canonical anchor.**

**Quentin Skinner, "Meaning and Understanding in the History of Ideas," *History and Theory* 8, no. 1 (1969): 3–53.**

- The original essay remains the more frequently cited form because it contains the original taxonomy of the four "mythologies" (mythology of doctrines, of coherence, of prolepsis, of parochialism). Both the 1969 essay and the revised 2002 chapter should be available to the skill author; the 2002 version is more authoritative but the 1969 is the historically operative document.

### Implementation / applied work (ranked)

**1. Quentin Skinner, *The Foundations of Modern Political Thought*, 2 vols. (Vol. 1: *The Renaissance*; Vol. 2: *The Age of Reformation*), Cambridge University Press, 1978 (ISBNs 9780521293372, 9780521294355).**

- The signature book-length application. Skinner reconstructs the political vocabulary of Renaissance and Reformation thinkers by situating each in its rhetorical and ideological context, treating texts as illocutionary moves. Volume 1 Part 2 (the Italian Renaissance — Machiavelli in context) and Volume 2 Part 3 (Calvinism and the theory of revolution) are the most demonstrative passages. Read for the moves Skinner actually makes when reconstructing intent.

**2. Quentin Skinner, *Liberty Before Liberalism*, Cambridge University Press, 1998 (ISBN 9780521631785), and *Hobbes and Republican Liberty*, Cambridge University Press, 2008 (ISBN 9780521714167).**

- Shorter and more pedagogically tractable than *Foundations*. *Liberty Before Liberalism* (≈140 pp.) excavates the neo-Roman theory of liberty from Milton, Harrington, Sidney, Nedham; the "Conclusion" is an explicit defense of the methodology. *Hobbes and Republican Liberty* extends this into a contrastive case study showing how Hobbes constructed his conception against republican opponents — a close-reading-of-illocutionary-acts at chapter length.

**3. James Tully (ed.), *Meaning and Context: Quentin Skinner and His Critics*, Polity Press, 1988 (ISBN 9780745604572).**

- Reprints five of Skinner's most important methodological articles, seven critical responses (including Charles Taylor's "The Hermeneutics of Conflict" and contributions by Hollis, Femia, Keane), and Skinner's 57-page reply. This is the operative textbook treatment because each Skinnerian move is paired with an objection and a Skinner counter-move — exactly the dialogical material a critic-mode skill needs. **For Mode 3 specifically, this is the single most usable single-volume source.**

**Adjacent Cambridge-school implementation**: J.G.A. Pocock, *The Machiavellian Moment* (Princeton, 1975); John Dunn, *The Political Thought of John Locke* (Cambridge, 1969). Both apply the contextualist method to specific corpora; Dunn's is closer in method to Skinner (intent-recovery), Pocock's relies more on tracking "languages" or paradigms across time and is methodologically distinct.

**Honest limitation**: No textbook-style step-by-step procedural treatment of Skinnerian reconstruction exists in the same sense Mode 1 has. Whatmore's *What Is Intellectual History?* (Polity, 2016) is the closest to a textbook, but it is more a discipline-survey than a how-to.

### Critique / limits sources (ranked)

**1. Mark Bevir, *The Logic of the History of Ideas*, Cambridge University Press, 1999 (ISBN 9780521640343).**

- The most systematic philosophical critique of Cambridge-school contextualism. Bevir argues for a "weak intentionalism" that distinguishes hermeneutic objectivity from Skinner's procedural intentionalism. Chapters 2 (meaning), 4 (belief), and 6 (diachronic explanation) are the operational critique chapters. The 2002 review symposium in *History of the Human Sciences* 15(2) supplies extension and counter-critique.

**2. Dominick LaCapra, *Rethinking Intellectual History: Texts, Contexts, Language*, Cornell University Press, 1983 (ISBN 9780801498862).**

- The continental-theoretic critique. LaCapra argues that Skinner's text/context distinction is naive — context is itself textual and requires its own interpretation — and pushes for an approach informed by deconstruction, dialogism (Bakhtin), and psychoanalysis. The introductory chapter and "Rethinking Intellectual History and Reading Texts" essay are the operative passages.

**3. James Tully (ed.), *Meaning and Context: Quentin Skinner and His Critics* (1988, full reference above) — recommended *also* as a critique source.**

- The seven critical essays cover the major objections: Charles Taylor on the unavailability of agent-perspective contexts; Joseph Femia's historicist critique; Martin Hollis's "Say it with Flowers" on the limits of speech-act analysis. This is double-counted because for Mode 3 it is uniquely useful as both implementation showcase and critique compilation.

### What each source contributes

- *Visions* Vol. 1 is the consolidated method statement; the 1969 essay supplies the original mythologies taxonomy in unrevised form.
- *Foundations* shows the method on a vast historical canvas; *Liberty Before Liberalism*/*Hobbes* shows it on a narrower, pedagogically tractable case.
- Bevir attacks the philosophical grounding (intentionalism, meaning); LaCapra attacks the text/context dichotomy from continental theory; Tully's volume aggregates the contemporary critiques and Skinner's own reply moves.

## MODE 4 — DENNETTIAN INTUITION-PUMP STRESS-TESTING

### Canonical primary source — VALIDATED

**Daniel C. Dennett, *Intuition Pumps and Other Tools for Thinking*, W. W. Norton, 2013 (ISBN 9780393082067 hardback / 9780393348781 paperback).**

- Confirmed canonical. The catalog of 77 thinking tools, organized into Parts II (twelve general tools — including Rapoport's Rules, Sturgeon's Law, Occam's Razor, the "deepity" diagnostic, "going meta," "jootsing," and turning the knobs on a thought experiment), III–V (meaning/content), VI (evolution), VII (consciousness, with explicit re-runs of zombies, Mary, the Chinese Room), and VIII (free will). The "turn the knobs" methodology in Part II is the directly extractable procedural core for a stress-testing skill. The appendix listing where each tool was first published is itself a roadmap to the rest of Dennett's corpus.

### Implementation / applied work (ranked)

**1. Daniel C. Dennett, *Consciousness Explained*, Little, Brown, 1991 (ISBN 9780316180665).**

- The single most extended application of intuition-pump stress-testing. Dennett's treatment of Searle's Chinese Room, philosophical zombies, Color Phi, Mary the color scientist, blindsight, and the Cartesian Theater provides the worked-example library that *Intuition Pumps* itself summarizes. Chapters 10 ("Show and Tell"), 11 ("Dismantling the Witness Protection Program"), 12 ("Qualia Disqualified"), and 14 (zombies) are the operational chapters. **For dialogical-move extraction specifically, this is the highest-value single source** because it shows Dennett doing the moves rather than describing them.

**2. Daniel C. Dennett, *Elbow Room: The Varieties of Free Will Worth Wanting*, MIT Press, 1984 (ISBN 9780262540421).**

- The book in which Dennett first uses "intuition pump" positively (as opposed to the pejorative 1980 deployment against Searle). The treatment of "the Invisible Jail" intuition pump, Sphex wasps, and the bugbears of determinism is the model for stress-testing intuition pumps in ethics/agency. Particularly useful for showing how to dismantle a misleading intuition pump rather than simply substitute one's own.

**3. Daniel C. Dennett, *Brainstorms: Philosophical Essays on Mind and Psychology*, MIT Press, 1978 (ISBN 9780262540377).**

- Earlier essays where many tools first appear ("Where Am I?", "Conditions of Personhood," "Why You Can't Make a Computer that Feels Pain"). Useful for showing the schemes in less polished, more obviously dialectical form. Lower priority than #1 and #2.

### Critique / limits sources (ranked)

**1. Herman Cappelen, *Philosophy without Intuitions*, Oxford University Press, 2012 (ISBN 9780199644865).**

- The most sustained recent challenge to the entire framing within which Dennett operates. Cappelen argues the assumption that analytic philosophers rely on intuitions as evidence is descriptively false. Part II ("The Argument from Philosophical Practice"), especially Chapter 8 (ten thought-experiment case studies), and Chapter 9 (comparison with Williamson) are the operational chapters. Useful even — and especially — for a Dennettian skill, because it reveals what the deflationary stance presupposes about its target.

**2. Timothy Williamson, *The Philosophy of Philosophy*, Blackwell, 2007 (ISBN 9781405133968).**

- Defends thought experiments (particularly Gettier cases) as yielding modal knowledge of counterfactuals rather than psychological reports of intuitions. Chapter 6 ("Thought Experiments") and Chapter 7 ("Evidence in Philosophy") are the operational chapters; Williamson's analysis of Gettier-style structure is the pedagogical workhorse. Together with Cappelen, the two main analytic-philosophical defenses of thought experiments against deflation.

**3. Joshua Knobe & Shaun Nichols (eds.), *Experimental Philosophy*, Oxford University Press, 2008 (ISBN 9780195323269), with sequel volume *Experimental Philosophy: Volume 2*, Oxford, 2013.**

- The experimental-philosophy challenge: empirical evidence that "philosophical intuitions" vary by demographic group, framing, and order. The "Experimental Philosophy Manifesto" (Knobe & Nichols) and the Weinberg/Nichols/Stich essay "Normativity and Epistemic Intuitions" supply the operational empirical critique. This both supports Dennett's deflation (intuitions are unreliable) and challenges his methodology (his intuition pumps are no more insulated from these effects than any others).

**Useful supplementary critique**: Elke Brendel, "Intuition Pumps and the Proper Use of Thought Experiments," *Dialectica* 58, no. 1 (2004): 89–108. This is article-length, not a book — included only as a pointer for the article-tier, since it is the most-cited article specifically diagnosing the difference between "bad intuition pumps" and legitimate thought experiments.

### What each source contributes

- *Intuition Pumps* gives the catalog and the "turn-the-knobs" procedure; *Consciousness Explained* and *Elbow Room* show the procedure executed on specific famous thought experiments.
- Cappelen attacks the descriptive premise (philosophers don't actually rely on intuitions in the way Dennett assumes); Williamson attacks the epistemological framing (intuitions aren't the relevant evidential category — counterfactual judgment is); experimental philosophy attacks the reliability of whatever-is-being-elicited empirically.

## CAVEATS

1. **Worked-example asymmetry across modes.** Modes 1 and 4 have well-developed textbook/applied literatures with explicit step-by-step worked examples. Modes 2 and 3 do not, in the strict sense; their canonical literature presents the methods as reflective practice and assumes the reader will internalize the moves through exposure to applied scholarship rather than through algorithmic pedagogy. For Mode 2 the closest substitute is Weinsheimer's chapter-by-chapter commentary on Gadamer plus Palmer's "Thirty Theses"; for Mode 3 it is the dialogical pairing in Tully's *Meaning and Context*. A skill-file author should expect to do more synthesis work for Modes 2 and 3 than for Modes 1 and 4.

2. **Skinner editions and the 1969 essay.** Skinner revised the 1969 essay substantially for *Visions of Politics* Vol. 1 (2002). For citation/quoting in skill rationale, prefer the 2002 chapter; for tracing the method's intellectual history, the 1969 *History and Theory* essay remains the document scholars cite. They are not interchangeable.

3. **Pragma-dialectics has updated since 2004.** Strategic maneuvering (van Eemeren & Houtlosser, then van Eemeren's 2010 monograph and the 2018 *Argumentation Theory*) is the framework's mature form. The 2004 *Systematic Theory* remains the canonical baseline because it is the integrated statement of the original ten-rule/four-stage framework, but a skill that aims at currency rather than canonicity should read the 2018 volume as well.

4. **Walton's *Argumentation Schemes* is a reference book, not a textbook.** It catalogs schemes with their critical questions but does not walk the reader through worked applications in the textbook sense; for that, *Methods of Argumentation* (2013) and *Fundamentals of Critical Argumentation* (2006) are needed. Some users mistakenly treat *Argumentation Schemes* as the implementation source — it is properly the canonical reference.

5. **Dennett, *Intuition Pumps* is partly a re-presentation.** Roughly a third of the tools in the 2013 book are revised excerpts from earlier Dennett works (the appendix lists provenance). This means a skill author who has the 2013 volume has the catalog but not always the original applied context; for some tools (e.g., heterophenomenology, Mary the color scientist) the *Consciousness Explained* original is more pedagogically rich.

6. **Mode 2 critique sources are older than the others by design.** The Habermas-Gadamer debate (1967–71), Hirsch (1967), and the Gadamer-Derrida encounter (1981) are the foundational critique literature; the user explicitly removed the recency constraint, but readers should be aware that contemporary hermeneutics (e.g., Vattimo, Caputo, Risser) has developed responses — Grondin (1994) is the most useful single source for that updated landscape.

7. **The Cambridge-school internal disputes (Mode 3) are real.** Pocock's "languages" methodology, while often grouped with Skinner's, differs in important respects (Pocock tracks discursive paradigms across long durée; Skinner reconstructs individual illocutionary intent). Skinner himself acknowledged Pocock's distinct path in his correspondence around the time of *Foundations* and *The Machiavellian Moment*. A Mode-3 skill that treats them as interchangeable will produce confused output; the skill file should distinguish "Skinnerian intent-recovery" from "Pocockian language-tracking."

8. **No source was excluded for paywall/availability reasons** — all listed titles are in print or available through standard academic library channels. A few have Internet Archive controlled-digital-lending availability (noted in passing during research), which can accelerate skill-authoring extraction.

9. **One category was deliberately *not* padded**: I did not include a third Mode-2 "implementation" textbook beyond Palmer, Weinsheimer, and Grondin, because the next-tier candidates (e.g., generic literary close-reading manuals, biblical hermeneutics primers) are not philosophically hermeneutic in the Gadamer/Ricoeur sense and would mislead a skill author looking to extract Gadamerian/Ricoeurian moves. The user's instruction to flag thin categories rather than pad applies here.
