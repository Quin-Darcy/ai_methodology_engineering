# 02-philosophy-exploratory-mode.md

## TL;DR

The literature for grounding a *generative/exploratory* philosophical interlocutor is thinner and more dispersed than the literature for the dialectical/critic mode. **There is no philosophical equivalent of Polya's *How to Solve It***: no canonical handbook of heuristics for the pre-argumentative phase of philosophical thinking. What exists instead is a layered patchwork: (a) ancient methodological resources (Aristotelian *aporia*/*diaporēsai*, exploratory readings of Socratic dialogue) that are theorized only in scholarly commentary rather than in primary how-to texts; (b) twentieth-century *dialogical* philosophy (Bohm, Gadamer, Buber) that theorizes joint inquiry but does not reduce it to method; (c) scattered methodological essays on intuition, conceptual analysis, thought experiments, and charity (Bealer, Sosa, Gendler, Sorensen, Jackson, Davidson) that bear on individual moves rather than on the whole exploratory disposition; and (d) a substantial body of *exemplary practice*—Wittgenstein's later remarks, Murdoch, Cavell, Anscombe, Parfit, Williams—where the disposition is exhibited rather than theorized. Recommendation: build the corpus around Dennett + Gadamer + Bohm as the methodological spine, supplement with Vlastos/Benson/Kahn for the Socratic-as-exploration angle and Aristotle's *Metaphysics* B for the *aporia*-as-survey model, and treat the exemplary-practice texts (Murdoch, Cavell, Wittgenstein's *Culture and Value*, Hadot) as ineliminable rather than ornamental — the disposition is communicable mostly by exhibition.

## 1. Methodology of Exploratory Philosophical Thinking

### What is and isn't available
There is no single canonical text for the "discovery phase" of philosophical work analogous to Polya's *How to Solve It*. The closest analogues are: (i) Dennett's *Intuition Pumps* (the existing baseline), which is explicitly framed as a toolkit; (ii) Gendler's methodological essays; (iii) the SEP entry on "Scientific Discovery" (Schickore) which explicitly notes that the "logic of discovery" tradition (Hanson, Lakatos, Darden) characterizes discovery in terms of *heuristic principles rather than inference rules*, and is broadly applicable to philosophy by analogy. The discovery/justification distinction (originating with Reichenbach) is well-known but, applied to philosophy specifically, is mostly handled in metaphilosophy literature (Williamson, Cappelen) which is not exploratory-disposition focused.

### Sources

- **Daniel C. Dennett, *Intuition Pumps and Other Tools for Thinking*** (W. W. Norton, 2013). [BASELINE — already in corpus.] *Type (i): explicitly theorizes exploratory tools.* Early chapters on Sturgeon's Law, Rapoport's Rules ("first re-express your target's position so clearly, vividly, and fairly that your target says, 'Thanks, I wish I'd thought of putting it that way'"), "surely" as a klaxon, deepities. Most direct match for "lightweight argument hygiene that does not require a thesis."

- **Tamar Szabó Gendler, *Intuition, Imagination, and Philosophical Methodology*** (Oxford University Press, 2010). *Type (i): theorizes exploratory phase.* Collects Gendler's essays on thought experiments, imaginative resistance, the role of intuition. Treats imagination as a generative philosophical capacity, not just an evidential one. Especially "Thought Experiments Rethought — and Reperceived" (2004) and "Personal Identity and Thought Experiments."

- **Timothy Williamson, *The Philosophy of Philosophy*** (Blackwell, 2007). *Type (i)* — but as a *foil* rather than support. Williamson explicitly defends armchair philosophy as continuous with empirical inquiry, downplaying the exceptionalism of philosophical method, and is skeptical that "intuitions" form a distinct evidentiary category. Useful as a check against over-romanticizing the "exploratory phase" as something special.

- **Pierre Hadot, *Philosophy as a Way of Life: Spiritual Exercises from Socrates to Foucault*** (ed. Arnold I. Davidson; trans. Michael Chase; Blackwell, 1995). *Type (ii)/(iii): theorizes ancient practice in a way that re-frames the generative phase as an exercise in attention and seeing.* Hadot's central thesis — that ancient philosophy was first a set of *exercises* on the self before being a body of doctrine — directly grounds the idea that philosophical work has a phase prior to argument-construction. Hadot's chapter "Forms of Life and Forms of Discourse in Ancient Philosophy" is the most relevant.

- **Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, "Scientific Discovery"** (Jutta Schickore). *Type (iv): adjacent.* Best concise survey of why heuristic principles for discovery are field-specific and not subject-neutral; corroborates the conjecture that there is unlikely to be a domain-neutral "Polya for philosophy."

- **James Pryor, "Guidelines on Reading Philosophy" and "Guidelines on Writing a Philosophy Paper"** (jimpryor.net). [BASELINE.] *Type (iv): pedagogical, adjacent.* Less about exploratory thinking per se, more about charitable structural reading — but the reading guidelines instruct exactly the disposition: figure out what the author means, give the strongest reading, separate auxiliary arguments from the main thread.

## 2. Dialogical Methods That Are Not Yet Dialectical

### What is and isn't available
This is the area of *richest* available literature, but the methodological lessons must mostly be extracted from primary works that are not framed as how-to manuals. The clearest single methodological text is Bohm's *On Dialogue*. The Socratic literature is mostly post-Vlastos *reaction* literature.

### Sources

#### Aristotelian *aporia* as exploration

- **Aristotle, *Metaphysics* Beta (Book III).** *Type (iii): exhibits the practice.* The locus classicus for treating philosophical work as the *survey of difficulties* (*diaporēsai*) before any thesis is offered. The Greek term sequence — *aporia* (impasse) → *diaporēsai* (working through both sides) → *euporia* (free advance) — is a methodological skeleton for the exploratory mode. Aristotle's framing at 995a24: "with a view to the science we are seeking, it is necessary that we should first review the things about which we need, from the outset, to be puzzled."

- **Michel Crubellier and André Laks (eds.), *Aristotle's Metaphysics Beta: Symposium Aristotelicum*** (Oxford University Press, 2009). *Type (i)/(ii): scholarly methodological commentary.* Nine essays going through the *aporiai* of Beta one by one. Friedemann Buddensiek's chapter (and his chapter "Aporia in Aristotle's *Metaphysics* Beta" in the Karamanolis/Politis volume below) is the most direct treatment of *aporia* as a *necessary* methodological stage rather than a failure.

- **George Karamanolis and Vasilis Politis (eds.), *The Aporetic Tradition in Ancient Philosophy*** (Cambridge University Press, 2017). *Type (i).* Whole-volume treatment of aporia as a positive exploratory method across Plato, Aristotle, the Academy, Pyrrhonism, Plotinus, and Damascius. Christof Rapp's chapter on the *Topics* characterizes aporia as a *dialectical problem* — a state of puzzlement resulting from reputable but conflicting views — i.e., a survey-of-endoxa method.

- **Nicholas Rescher, *Aporetics: Rational Deliberation in the Face of Inconsistency*** (University of Pittsburgh Press, 2009). *Type (i).* A contemporary analytic treatment of reasoning under inconsistent commitments. Useful methodological framework when the interlocutor is sitting in a contradiction they have not yet resolved.

#### Socratic method as joint inquiry rather than refutation

- **Gregory Vlastos, *Socrates: Ironist and Moral Philosopher*** (Cornell University Press, 1991). *Type (i)/(ii): theorizes Socratic method.* The standard reference for the "elenchus as truth-seeking" reading. Even where Vlastos over-narrows Socratic method to elenchus, his 1956 introduction to the *Protagoras* characterizes the elenchus's function as the increase of "insight into the logical relations between propositions" — a description nearly identical to charitable exploratory engagement.

- **Vlastos, "The Socratic Elenchus" (1983), *Oxford Studies in Ancient Philosophy* 1: 27–58, with "Afterthoughts on the Socratic Elenchus," same volume, 71–74.** *Type (i).* The article that defined the field for a generation; necessary background for Benson, Kahn, Scott.

- **Hugh H. Benson, *Socratic Wisdom: The Model of Knowledge in Plato's Early Dialogues*** (Oxford University Press, 2000). *Type (i).* Treats elenchus as embedded in a *positive epistemology* — it is part of how one comes to a better state of "still not knowing what to say," which is improved knowledge, not refutation per se. Most useful for the AI use case: Benson's reading makes elenchus look less adversarial and more like joint diagnostic work.

- **Charles H. Kahn, *Plato and the Socratic Dialogue: The Philosophical Use of a Literary Form*** (Cambridge University Press, 1996). *Type (i).* Kahn's "ingressive" reading: the early dialogues are not refutational artifacts but a deliberate *gradual indirect exposition* preparing the reader for a positive view. Directly supports treating Socratic conversation as exploratory and pedagogical rather than refutational.

- **Gary Alan Scott (ed.), *Does Socrates Have a Method? Rethinking the Elenchus in Plato's Dialogues and Beyond*** (Pennsylvania State University Press, 2002). *Type (i).* The post-Vlastos volume. Multiple contributors argue Vlastos's identification of Socratic method with "the elenchus" is too narrow; Socrates is doing something more versatile and less refutation-focused.

#### Modern dialogical philosophy

- **David Bohm, *On Dialogue*** (ed. Lee Nichol; Routledge, 1996; 2nd ed. 2004). *Type (i): theorizes dialogue as joint inquiry.* The single most direct match for the disposition the brief specifies. Bohm distinguishes dialogue (collaborative exploration of meaning) from discussion (which shares a root with "percussion" — break apart). Core concepts: *suspension of assumptions* (neither carry out nor suppress them), *proprioception of thought*, *no predefined purpose or agenda*. The disposition Bohm describes — "relaxed, nonjudgmental curiosity, its primary activity being to see things as freshly and clearly as possible" — is essentially a description of the target disposition. **TIER 1.**

- **Hans-Georg Gadamer, *Truth and Method*** (1960; 2nd revised English edition trans. Joel Weinsheimer and Donald G. Marshall; Continuum, 1989/Bloomsbury, 2004). [BASELINE.] *Type (i)/(ii): theorizes understanding as dialogical.* Part II's treatment of the *fusion of horizons* (*Horizontverschmelzung*) and Gadamer's positive account of prejudice (*Vorurteil*) as *condition* of understanding rather than its enemy. Pairs naturally with Bohm. The hermeneutic stance — questioning, listening, allowing the text or interlocutor to make a claim on you — is the disposition.

- **Hans-Georg Gadamer, "The Incapacity for Conversation"** (1971), trans. David Vessey and Chris Blauwkamp, *Continental Philosophy Review* 39 (2006): 351–359. *Type (i).* Short essay diagnosing what *prevents* genuine dialogue (specialism, monological posture, refusal to risk one's prejudices). Useful as a negative characterization of what the AI should *not* do.

- **Martin Buber, *I and Thou*** (1923; trans. Walter Kaufmann, Scribner, 1970, or Ronald Gregor Smith, T&T Clark, 1937). *Type (i)/(iii).* The I–Thou / I–It distinction. Buber's notion of *confirmation* — acknowledging another's unique being including their potential — and his trichotomy of dialogue forms (genuine dialogue / technical dialogue / monologue disguised as dialogue) are directly applicable. *I and Thou* is a poetic/programmatic text; *Between Man and Man* (1947) is the more methodologically useful companion.

- **Martin Buber, *Between Man and Man*** (1947; trans. Ronald Gregor Smith, Routledge, 2002). *Type (i).* Includes "Dialogue" and "What is Man?" — clearer prose than *I and Thou* and explicitly methodological.

- **Stanley Cavell, *The Claim of Reason: Wittgenstein, Skepticism, Morality, and Tragedy*** (Oxford University Press, 1979). *Type (ii)/(iii): theorizes and exhibits.* Especially Part IV, "Skepticism and the Problem of Others: Between Acknowledgment and Avoidance." Cavell's notion of *acknowledgment* (vs. *knowing*) is the philosophical-ethical analogue of charitable engagement.

- **Stanley Cavell, *Must We Mean What We Say?*** (Cambridge University Press, 1969; updated edition 2002). *Type (iii).* The title essay and "The Availability of Wittgenstein's Later Philosophy" exhibit Cavell's "linguistic phenomenology" — using oneself as a source of philosophical evidence by asking "what we say when…" — which is exactly the move of taking inchoate intuition seriously.

## 3. Charitable Engagement with Vague Intuitions and Ill-Formed Thoughts

### What is and isn't available
The *principle of charity* (Davidson, Quine, Wilson) is well-codified for interpretation of *speech*. Its extension into "charitable engagement with what is not yet articulated" is more diffuse — handled by Cavell on acknowledgment, by phenomenologists on pre-reflective awareness, and by the "intuition as evidence" literature.

### Sources

- **Donald Davidson, "Radical Interpretation"** (1973), in *Inquiries into Truth and Interpretation* (Oxford University Press, 1984; 2nd ed. 2001). *Type (i).* The locus classicus for the principle of charity as a *constitutive* condition of interpretation: "charity is forced on us; whether we like it or not, if we want to understand others, we must count them right in most matters." Davidson explicitly refers to it as the principle of "rational accommodation."

- **W. V. O. Quine, *Word and Object*** (MIT Press, 1960), §13. *Type (i).* The earlier source: "one's interlocutor's silliness, beyond a certain point, is less likely than bad translation."

- **Neil L. Wilson, "Substances Without Substrata,"** *Review of Metaphysics* 12 (1959): 521–539. *Type (i).* Where the principle was first explicitly named.

- **George Bealer, "A Priori Knowledge and the Scope of Philosophy,"** *Philosophical Studies* 81 (1996): 121–142, and "Intuition and the Autonomy of Philosophy," in *Rethinking Intuition* (eds. Michael DePaul and William Ramsey; Rowman & Littlefield, 1998), 201–240. *Type (i).* Bealer argues intuitions are *intellectual seemings* with evidential status; the rejection of intuition is self-defeating. Grounds the practice of taking the interlocutor's pre-articulate seemings seriously.

- **Ernest Sosa, "Minimal Intuition,"** in DePaul and Ramsey (eds.) *Rethinking Intuition* (1998), 257–270, and Sosa's later work in *A Virtue Epistemology* (Oxford University Press, 2007). *Type (i).* Sosa develops the notion of intuition as a *seeming* whose content is the proposition that seems true on competent consideration alone — useful for the practice of helping someone articulate what they "find themselves wanting to say."

- **Herman Cappelen, *Philosophy Without Intuitions*** (Oxford University Press, 2012). *Type (i): foil.* Argues that contemporary analytic philosophy relies less on "intuitions" than the standard story claims. Useful as a corrective; suggests that what looks like intuition-talk is often ordinary case-based judgment, which the AI can engage with directly.

- **Ludwig Wittgenstein, *Philosophical Investigations*** (1953; 4th ed. trans. G. E. M. Anscombe, P. M. S. Hacker, J. Schulte; Wiley-Blackwell, 2009). *Type (iii): exhibits.* The later Wittgenstein's method is the primary working model for taking inchoate utterance seriously and helping articulate what is half-said. Especially §122 (*übersichtliche Darstellung*, "perspicuous/surveyable representation" — "a main source of our failure to understand is that we do not command a clear view of the use of our words … A perspicuous representation produces just that understanding which consists in 'seeing connexions'") and §§89–133 on philosophical method.

- **Stanley Cavell, "The Availability of Wittgenstein's Later Philosophy"** in *Must We Mean What We Say?* (1969). *Type (i)/(iii).* Best methodological gloss on Wittgenstein's exploratory practice for our purposes — Cavell makes explicit the relationship between articulation, voice, and the difficulty of saying what one means.

- **Maurice Merleau-Ponty, *Phenomenology of Perception*** (1945; trans. Donald A. Landes, Routledge, 2012). *Type (iii) and adjacent (iv).* The Preface and the "tacit cogito"/"spoken cogito" distinction in Part III, ch. 1, ground the idea that articulation is the *expressive bringing-to-language* of pre-reflective sense, not the reading-off of an antecedent thought. The phenomenological tradition (Husserl, Merleau-Ponty, Henry, Zahavi) on pre-reflective awareness is the philosophical home of "the half-formed thought is already cognitively meaningful before being articulated."

- **Wilfrid Sellars, "Empiricism and the Philosophy of Mind"** (1956; book ed. with intro by Richard Rorty and study guide by Robert Brandom, Harvard University Press, 1997). *Type (i)/(iv).* The "Myth of the Given" critique. Methodologically relevant in a *negative* sense for our use case: it warns against treating pre-articulate experience as a self-justifying epistemic foundation. The exploratory disposition should *take seriously* inchoate intuition without treating it as inerrant — Sellars provides the discipline for that.

- **Charles Taylor, "What is Human Agency?"** and "Self-Interpreting Animals," in *Human Agency and Language: Philosophical Papers 1* (Cambridge University Press, 1985). *Type (i).* Taylor's account of articulation as *constitutive* — putting feelings and intuitions into words partly *makes* them what they are — is the most useful philosophical theorization of the "help articulate vague intuitions" function the AI is meant to perform.

## 4. Lightweight Argument Hygiene That Does Not Require a Thesis

### Sources

- **Daniel C. Dennett, *Intuition Pumps and Other Tools for Thinking*** (W. W. Norton, 2013), Part I esp. chs. 3–11. [BASELINE.] *Type (i).* Sturgeon's Law, Rapoport's Rules, the use of "surely" as a klaxon for assumptions doing illicit work, "deepities" (statements that look profound by trading on ambiguity), the *occam's broom* (sweeping inconvenient facts under the rug). These tools work without a positive thesis — they are diagnostic of how thinking is going.

- **Stephen Toulmin, *The Uses of Argument*** (Cambridge University Press, 1958; updated edition 2003). *Type (i).* The DWC layout (data → warrant → claim, with backing, qualifier, rebuttal). Useful for the AI in *un-bundling* what the interlocutor is saying — separating "the claim you're making" from "the move you're using to support it" from "the assumption that licenses that move." Field-dependent rather than universal — Toulmin's central methodological point ("what counts as convincing may vary substantially between the law court, the laboratory and the debating chamber") fits the brief's anti-pseudo-precision norm.

- **Douglas Walton, *Informal Logic: A Pragmatic Approach*** (2nd ed., Cambridge University Press, 2008), and Walton, Reed, and Macagno, ***Argumentation Schemes*** (Cambridge University Press, 2008). *Type (i)/(iv).* Walton's *argumentation schemes* (96 in the 2008 compendium: argument from expert opinion, from analogy, from sign, etc.) come paired with *critical questions* — and the critical questions are exactly the lightweight hygiene moves the brief calls for ("here is where this might run into trouble"). Walton's framework treats fallacies not as automatically bad moves but as defeasible patterns whose appropriateness depends on context.

- **Trudy Govier, *A Practical Study of Argument*** (1st ed. 1985; 7th ed. Cengage, 2010). *Type (iv): pedagogical.* The standard informal-logic textbook in the Govier tradition. Usable as a reference of moves; less methodologically distinctive than Walton.

- **Frank Jackson, *From Metaphysics to Ethics: A Defence of Conceptual Analysis*** (Oxford University Press, 1998). *Type (i).* Defends conceptual analysis as a *modest* methodology — not a search for necessary-and-sufficient conditions in the old style, but a way of mapping commitments and connections among concepts. Pairs well with Toulmin: both authorize lightweight, partial mapping rather than systematic theory-building.

- **John Rawls, "Outline of a Decision Procedure for Ethics,"** *Philosophical Review* 60 (1951): 177–197, and *A Theory of Justice* §9 (1971). *Type (i): adjacent.* Rawls's *reflective equilibrium* method is, in its low-key procedural form, a description of the lightweight hygiene the brief calls for: bringing intuitions and principles into mutual adjustment without a fixed prior thesis.

## 5. Imagination, Conjecture, and "Philosophical Taste" in Early-Stage Work

### Sources

- **Roy A. Sorensen, *Thought Experiments*** (Oxford University Press, 1992; paperback 1998). *Type (i).* The first general theory of thought experiments. Sorensen treats them as "a subset of unexecuted experiments" with the logical structure of paradoxes that test modal consequences. Distinguishes "Necessity Refuters" from "Possibility Refuters." Most useful for the AI use case: provides a vocabulary for describing what a thought experiment is *doing* generatively.

- **Tamar Szabó Gendler, *Thought Experiment: On the Powers and Limits of Imaginary Cases*** (Routledge/Garland, 2000). *Type (i).* Argues thought experiments work by forcing us to make sense of *exceptional cases* — cases where ordinarily co-instantiated patterns of features come apart. Methodologically central for the generative phase.

- **Tamar Szabó Gendler, *Intuition, Imagination, and Philosophical Methodology*** (Oxford University Press, 2010) — see §1 above.

- **James Robert Brown, *The Laboratory of the Mind: Thought Experiments in the Natural Sciences*** (Routledge, 1991; 2nd ed. 2011). *Type (i).* Platonist account; useful as a foil to Sorensen and Gendler.

- **Bernard Williams, *Ethics and the Limits of Philosophy*** (Harvard University Press / Fontana, 1985; Routledge Classics 2011, with commentary by A. W. Moore). *Type (iii)/(i).* Williams's recurring methodological move: distrust of moral *theory*, attention to the texture of moral life, suspicion of "system" as an end in itself. Williams's critique of *the morality system* and of the disengaged Archimedean point ("the view from nowhere") is a sustained example of philosophical work pursued in exploratory mode rather than thesis-defense mode.

- **Iris Murdoch, *The Sovereignty of Good*** (Routledge & Kegan Paul, 1970; reissued Routledge Classics 2001 with Mary Midgley intro). *Type (iii).* "The Idea of Perfection" in particular — the famous M/D example (mother-in-law and daughter-in-law) — exhibits the disposition the brief calls for: attention as moral and philosophical work, the slow re-seeing of a particular, the refusal of premature theorization. Murdoch's *attention* (drawn from Simone Weil) is the closest moral-philosophical analogue of the charitable engagement the AI should embody.

- **Iris Murdoch, *Existentialists and Mystics: Writings on Philosophy and Literature*** (ed. Peter Conradi; Allen Lane/Penguin, 1997; Penguin Classics 1999). *Type (iii).* Especially "The Idea of Perfection," "Against Dryness," "On 'God' and 'Good'," "Vision and Choice in Morality."

- **Ludwig Wittgenstein, *Culture and Value*** (ed. Georg Henrik von Wright with Heikki Nyman; rev. Alois Pichler; trans. Peter Winch; Wiley-Blackwell, 1998). *Type (iii).* Wittgenstein's remarks on philosophical method, on the importance of style, on examples ("It is so difficult to find the *beginning*. Or, better: it is difficult to begin at the beginning. And not try to go further back."). The closest thing in the corpus to "philosophical taste" theorized by the practitioner.

- **Wittgenstein, *Philosophical Investigations*** (op. cit.), §§89–133, on philosophical method (assembling reminders, perspicuous representation, the fly-bottle, philosophy as therapy). *Type (iii).*

- **Arthur Schopenhauer (mentioned for completeness), Marcel Proust, and the broader tradition of philosophy-as-perception** — *adjacent (iv).* The brief mentions Murdoch on "attention and moral perception as a model of philosophical seeing"; Schopenhauer on contemplation and Proust's account of the involuntary cognition of significance are background influences on Murdoch but are not core sources.

## 6. Pedagogical and Conversational Sources; Working Philosophers' Practice

### What is and isn't available
This is where the corpus must lean most heavily on *exemplary practice* (type iii) because explicit how-to-do-philosophy texts by major working philosophers are rare. The Adler line is the clearest pedagogical tradition; Wittgenstein, Murdoch, Cavell, and Anscombe are the clearest practice exemplars; Hadot is the clearest methodological retrieval of the older tradition.

### Sources

- **Mortimer J. Adler and Charles Van Doren, *How to Read a Book: The Classic Guide to Intelligent Reading*** (Simon & Schuster, rev. ed. 1972). *Type (iv): pedagogical.* Levels of reading (elementary → inspectional → analytical → syntopical) — the *analytical* level explicitly enjoins the reader to find the author's questions, not just their answers, and to charitably re-state the author's argument before criticizing. Adler's rule of charitable understanding ("you must be able to say, with reasonable certainty, 'I understand,' before you can say any one of the following: 'I agree,' 'I disagree,' or 'I suspend judgment'") is methodologically central.

- **Mortimer J. Adler, *How to Speak, How to Listen*** (Macmillan, 1983). *Type (iv).* The most direct Adler treatment of conversation. Less well-known than *How to Read a Book*, but more directly applicable to dialogue rather than reading.

- **Mortimer J. Adler, *The Paideia Proposal: An Educational Manifesto*** (Macmillan, 1982). *Type (iv).* The three-column Paideia model — didactic instruction / coaching / Socratic seminar — and the explicit defense of *Socratic seminar* as the third pillar of education. Adler's "Socratic seminar" is exploratory rather than refutational: questions designed to draw out understanding rather than to test it.

- **Bertrand Russell, *The Problems of Philosophy*** (Williams & Norgate / Home University Library, 1912). *Type (iii).* An exhibit of philosophy done as careful exploration: each chapter takes up a problem, surveys what has been said, and works through the difficulties without insisting on a thesis. Russell's prose is the model of low-formality exploratory writing.

- **G. E. M. Anscombe, "Modern Moral Philosophy,"** *Philosophy* 33 (1958): 1–19. *Type (iii).* The negative case — Anscombe is doing critic-mode work in this essay — but her *positive* methodological move (one cannot do moral philosophy adequately without first doing philosophy of psychology; the concepts of "obligation" and "duty" need to be set aside as historically conditioned residues) is itself an example of philosophical exploration: she steps back from the conventional vocabulary to ask whether the question has been well-formed at all.

- **G. E. M. Anscombe, *Intention*** (Harvard University Press, 1957; 2nd ed. 1963). *Type (iii).* Working slowly, by closely attended examples, building up a picture without an early thesis. Among the clearest exhibits of philosophical exploration in twentieth-century analytic philosophy.

- **Stanley Cavell, *A Pitch of Philosophy: Autobiographical Exercises*** (Harvard University Press, 1994). *Type (iii).* Cavell's autobiographical writing on what philosophy *felt like* to do — the search for voice, the difficulty of saying what one means.

- **Stanley Cavell, *The Claim of Reason*** (op. cit., §2 above). The Introduction and Part IV are the most exploratory.

- **Derek Parfit, *Reasons and Persons*** (Oxford University Press, 1984), and David Edmonds, *Parfit: A Philosopher and His Mission to Save Morality* (Princeton University Press, 2023). *Type (iii) (Parfit) + biographical/methodological commentary (Edmonds).* Parfit's working method, as documented by Edmonds, was distinctive: extensive redrafting, soliciting comments from many other philosophers, treating philosophy as a long collaborative conversation rather than a solitary thesis-defense. The *Reasons and Persons* manuscript was reportedly rewritten dozens of times. Edmonds also notes Parfit's exceptionally generous practice of giving extensive comments on others' work — i.e., Parfit modeled charitable critical engagement at scale. The "Climbing the Mountain" metaphor governing *On What Matters* (different ethical traditions ascending the same mountain on different sides) is itself an exploratory-mode framing of philosophical disagreement.

- **Ludwig Wittgenstein, *Culture and Value*** (op. cit., §5). *Type (iii).*

- **Iris Murdoch, *The Sovereignty of Good*** and *Existentialists and Mystics* (op. cit., §5). *Type (iii).*

- **Pierre Hadot, *Philosophy as a Way of Life*** (op. cit., §1) and *What is Ancient Philosophy?* (trans. Michael Chase; Harvard University Press / Belknap, 2002). *Type (i).* Hadot's reframing of ancient philosophy as a *practice* before a doctrine — and his attention to the figure of Socrates as a midwife rather than a refuter — directly supports the exploratory-disposition reading.

- **Richard Rorty, *Philosophy and the Mirror of Nature*** (Princeton University Press, 1979), Part III ("Philosophy"), esp. ch. 8 "Philosophy Without Mirrors." *Type (i).* The argument for *edifying* (vs. *systematic*) philosophy, and for "philosophy as conversation" rather than philosophy as foundation-laying. Useful programmatically, even if one rejects Rorty's wider anti-representationalism — the conversational ideal is articulated here more programmatically than anywhere else.

- **Alasdair MacIntyre, *Whose Justice? Which Rationality?*** (University of Notre Dame Press, 1988), and *Three Rival Versions of Moral Enquiry* (University of Notre Dame Press, 1990). *Type (i).* MacIntyre's *tradition-constituted inquiry*: rationality is always already embedded in a tradition, and engagement with another tradition is a form of conversation that proceeds by identifying the other tradition's *epistemological crises* charitably (i.e., from inside its own resources). This is a substantive philosophical theorization of charitable engagement across frameworks.

- **Bernard Williams, "Philosophy as a Humanistic Discipline,"** *Philosophy* 75 (2000): 477–496, repr. in *Philosophy as a Humanistic Discipline* (ed. A. W. Moore; Princeton University Press, 2006). *Type (i)/(iii).* Williams's late methodological essay. The clearest single Williams text on what philosophy *is* methodologically when it is not aping science — useful pair with *Ethics and the Limits of Philosophy*.

## Caveats

1. **The literature is genuinely thin in the area the brief targets most directly.** There is no philosophical *How to Solve It*. Anyone constructing the corpus should not pretend otherwise. The closest analogue we have — Dennett's *Intuition Pumps* — is itself relatively recent (2013), self-consciously informal, and explicitly sceptical that systematic methodology is possible. The deeper sources (Aristotle's *aporia*, the later Wittgenstein, Murdoch on attention, Bohm on dialogue) all *exhibit* or *gesture at* the disposition more than they codify it. This is not a defect of the research; it is a fact about the literature.

2. **Most "Socratic method" literature is about elenchus.** The brief explicitly asks for sources that distinguish *exploratory* Socratic dialogue from *refutational* elenchus. Such sources exist (Benson, Kahn, the Scott volume, parts of Vlastos), but the dominant frame in both the primary texts and the secondary scholarship is still elenchus-as-refutation. The exploratory reading of Socratic dialogue is a *minority* reading that has to be reconstructed from these sources rather than extracted whole.

3. **The strongest sources are exemplary practice rather than methodological theory.** Wittgenstein, Murdoch, Cavell, Anscombe, Parfit, and Williams *do* the thing the AI is supposed to do — they engage charitably with vague intuitions, surface implicit assumptions without weaponizing them, refuse pseudo-precision, push back tentatively rather than adversarially. They mostly do not theorize this practice. Treat them as exhibits. The disposition seems to be communicable largely by exhibition rather than by codification — which is itself a methodological finding.

4. **Bohm is methodologically the closest single match, but he is a physicist-mystic, not a professional philosopher.** *On Dialogue* sometimes shades into therapeutic-spiritual territory that may not transfer to a philosophical-AI use case without some editing. Use the *Wholeness and the Implicate Order*-adjacent chapters cautiously; the core methodology (suspension of assumptions, proprioception of thought, the distinction between dialogue and discussion) is robust and transferable.

5. **The principle-of-charity literature is narrowly about *speech interpretation* in the philosophy of language.** Davidson and Quine codify charity as a constraint on radical interpretation — i.e., on how to assign meanings to alien utterances. Extending charity to "engaging well with someone's half-formed intuition" is not a straightforward inference; it requires the bridge work that Cavell (acknowledgment), Taylor (articulation), and the phenomenologists (pre-reflective awareness) provide. Do not assume the principle of charity by itself does the methodological work.

6. **Several sources are described above from general/scholarly knowledge plus search-snippet verification rather than from full direct reading.** Specifically: the precise content of Rescher's *Aporetics*, the Karamanolis/Politis volume's individual chapters, Walton's *Argumentation Schemes* compendium contents, Charles Taylor's two essays, Williams's "Philosophy as a Humanistic Discipline," and Cavell's *A Pitch of Philosophy* are characterized from snippets and standard knowledge. Bibliographic data (publisher, year, edition) should be re-verified before any of these enter a final citation list.

7. **Parfit's "working method" should be cited carefully.** The Edmonds biography reports it; some accounts of Parfit's practice are anecdotal. The defensible claims are: (i) extensive multi-decade redrafting of *Reasons and Persons* and *On What Matters*; (ii) exceptionally generous practice of commenting on others' drafts; (iii) the "Climbing the Mountain" framing (which appears in *On What Matters* itself, not just secondhand). Avoid making stronger claims about a Parfit "method."

8. **Watch the distinction between dialogue-as-philosophical-method and dialogue-as-spiritual-practice.** Buber and Bohm both straddle the line; Hadot explicitly *re-fuses* them. For the AI's purposes, the methodological core — suspension, listening, charity, joint inquiry, refusal of premature closure — survives the distinction. But sources should be read with the use case in mind, not adopted wholesale.

9. **The corpus skews Western and analytic-or-continental.** There are non-Western dialogical traditions (Confucian *zhengming* / rectification of names; Buddhist *catuṣkoṭi*; the *talmudic* method of *machloket l'shem shamayim*) that exhibit relevantly similar dispositions but are out of scope of this brief and have not been surveyed here. Flag for possible follow-up if the project widens.

10. **Subagent budget was held in reserve and not deployed for a separate gap-filling pass within this research session; the thinnest area (working philosophers' explicit descriptions of their own *generative* method) remains the area where the corpus is most reliant on biographical/secondary sources rather than first-person methodological essays. The Edmonds biography of Parfit, Cavell's autobiographical writing, and Wittgenstein's *Culture and Value* are the best partial substitutes available, but a serious corpus-builder should consider supplementing with interviews and prefaces (e.g., the *Philosophy Bites* corpus, the *Harvard Review of Philosophy* interviews, prefaces to major works) to thicken this area.**
