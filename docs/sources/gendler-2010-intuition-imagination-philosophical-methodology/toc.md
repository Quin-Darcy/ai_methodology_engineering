## Preface (excerpt — what the volume is)

> The fourteen essays collected in this volume explore a trio of interrelated themes. The first is intuition: what are the powers and limits of appeals to intuition in supporting or refuting various sorts of claims? The second is imagination: what are the cognitive consequences of engaging with content that is represented as unreal? The third is philosophical methodology: what are the implications of these issues for the methodology of philosophy more generally? These themes are explored in a variety of cases, including thought experiments in science and philosophy, early childhood pretense, self-deception, cognitive and emotional engagement with fiction, mental and motor imagery, automatic and habitual behavior, and social categorization.

> In addition to the thematic connections, the essays also share a methodological outlook. Each draws not only on traditional and contemporary philosophical work, but also on related work in empirical psychology. One of the volume's main goals is to demonstrate the numerous ways in which these approaches can be mutually illuminating.

The volume's distinctive methodological claim is that thought experiments and intuitions cannot be evaluated philosophically without engagement with the empirical psychology of how the imagination actually functions — the volume thereby occupies a middle position between the experimental-philosophy critics of intuitions (e.g., Cappelen) and the rationalist defenders (e.g., Williamson, BonJour).

## Operational map for the exploratory-default deliverable

Per the manifest, the operational target is **Ch. 2 ("Thought Experiments Rethought — and Reperceived", pp. 42–52)**. This essay's argument — that running a thought experiment is *not* a matter of consulting a faculty of pure rational insight but a matter of mentally simulating the scenario and reporting what one perceives — gives the exploratory disposition its account of what thought experiments are *doing* when invoked, and a check on how they can mislead.

Beyond Ch. 2, several other chapters are directly transferable to the exploratory disposition:

- **Ch. 1 ("Galileo and the Indispensability of Scientific Thought Experiment", pp. 21–41)** — sets up the case for thought experiments as cognitively contentful (not merely rhetorical), using Galileo's Pisa argument as the worked case. Useful for grounding the disposition's stance toward hypotheticals.
- **Ch. 3 ("Exceptional Persons: On the Limits of Imaginary Cases", pp. 53–73)** — limits-of-thought-experiments analysis; pairs naturally with Mode 4 (Dennettian critic) work.
- **Ch. 5 ("The Real Guide to Fake Barns", pp. 98–115, with Hawthorne)** — an applied catalogue showing how thought experiments degrade when the imaginative load they carry is misjudged. Useful for the exploratory disposition's hygiene around hypothetical cases.
- **Ch. 6 ("Philosophical Thought Experiments, Intuitions, and Cognitive Equilibrium", pp. 116–134)** — directly about *philosophical* methodology; the most general statement of the volume's position on what intuitions provide.

For directive extraction, **Chs. 2 and 6** are the primary candidates; Ch. 1 is the indispensable contextualization. Chunking by chapter is the natural granularity (each chapter is ~10–25 pages).

This source has cross-relevance to **Mode 4 (Dennettian critic)** since Dennett's "intuition pump" framing pairs directly with Gendler's "thought-experiments-as-mental-simulation" account; Chs. 1, 2, 3, 5, 6 are the main contact points. The Tier 3 entry `cappelen-2012-philosophy-without-intuitions` (the eliminativist counter-position) reads naturally against Gendler's Part I.
