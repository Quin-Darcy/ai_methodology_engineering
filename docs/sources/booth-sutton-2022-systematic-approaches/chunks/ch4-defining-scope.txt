         DEFINING YOUR SCOPE

                 in a nutshell

How to define your review scope
• A good review begins with an answerable research question. Consider using a framework
  to structure and define the key concepts of your research.
• When you set out the aims of your research at the outset, you improve your chances of
  being successful.
• When you are clear about what is (and what is not!) within your scope you will find it easier
  to search the literature and to select appropriate studies.
• Completing a review protocol when defining your scope makes it easier to plan your review.
• If you use processes such as descriptive mapping, data mining, concept mapping and logic
  models to characterise the available literature you are able to refine your review scope and
  to choose an appropriate review method.
• When defining your scope you can anticipate and avoid common pitfalls, which could otherwise slow down or derail your review.

                  key terms explained

   complex intervention: intervention with multiple components and/or mediators and
   moderators of effect. Complex interventions may target multiple participants, groups or
   organisational levels; require multifaceted adoption, uptake or integration strategies; or
   work in a dynamic multi-dimensional environment.

=== p. 94 ===

        concept mapping: visual organisation and representation of knowledge that shows concepts and ideas and the relationships among them. You create a concept map by writing
        keywords (sometimes within shapes, e.g. circles, boxes, triangles, etc.) and then drawing
        arrows between related ideas.

        logic model: diagram or flowchart that conveys relationships between contextual factors,
        inputs, processes and outcomes. Logic models are considered valuable in illustrating influential relationships and components from inputs to outcomes.

        PICO(S): mnemonic used to describe the four elements of well-formulated question for
        a review of effectiveness: Population/Person/Patient/Problem–Intervention–Comparison–
        Outcome-Study type(s). Compare SPICE for qualitative review questions.

        review protocol: document that formally lays out in advance how you will approach each
        step of your review process. The review protocol serves to protect against bias, set the
        rules for the conduct of the review and stake your claim to the topic.

INTRODUCTION
So, you have an idea for a topic you would like to review. You have an intended audience and
purpose in mind. But where do you start?
     You need to make three decisions, all of which are related:

1.     On what specific aspect of the topic will you focus your review?
2.     What topics and/or types of evidence should you include and exclude?
3.     What type of review are you going to conduct?

Key factors when deciding your scope are how much resource (time, person power, financial
resources, expertise) you have available (Pullin et al., 2018). Are you working alone or in a
team? How much time do you have to complete the review? How much evidence can you
reasonably manage?
     This chapter helps you to make informed choices as you plan your review.

Why is defining your scope important?
Developing your review question is critical to the research process (Thomas et al., 2019) and is
as important for you as author as it is for future readers of your review.
     As for any research, when planning a review we benefit from setting objectives that are specific; measurable; achievable; realistic and time-limited (SMART) (as opposed to being vague and
aspirational) (Alves, 2018). For your review to be useful, your audience must be confident that
you have answered the question you set out to address and be clear on what you have covered
as well as what you chose to omit (and why).
     When you define your scope you set clear boundaries around your topic. You reduce the risk
of making subjective decisions in deciding what is relevant. Defining your scope also helps you

=== p. 95 ===

to be consistent throughout your review and to ensure that you deliver what you have promised
to your audience.
    Typically, you define your scope after an initial estimate of the size and nature of the relevant literature, based on a scoping search (see Chapter 5). A scoping search is less exhaustive than a full, systematic search but helps to estimate how many papers you will have to manage within your review.
    If you do not undertake a scoping search you may encounter problems later in the review,
for example:

•     You might over-promise by committing yourself to a review for which you do not have sufficient resources to manage all eligible studies.
•     You might commit to reviewing a topic for which no evidence exists (or set yourself inclusion
      criteria which are so restrictive that no studies remain eligible for review) – an ‘empty review’.
•     You might commit to reviewing a topic in which a high-quality, comprehensive, recent
      review already exists – making all your hard work redundant.

By scoping your topic properly before writing your protocol, you should have an approximate
idea of how much literature and what previous reviews exist on similar topics, so that you can
target a unique ‘niche’ for your own review.
    Can you realistically answer your research question within a single review? How will you
know for sure that you have identified all the relevant evidence? Following the example in
Exercise 4.1 below, try to develop a SMART question for your own review.

=== p. 96 ===

      The three elements of defining the scope

         WHO = who is the research question about?

         WHAT = what must I find out to answer the research question?

         HOW = how will the study impact on the ‘who’? (what is the outcome?)

         Adapted from Ibrahim (2008).

In short, defining your scope takes you from:

       ‘I’d quite like to do some research on under-age drinking …’

to:

       ‘I’m going to review the literature on

       the economic and social implications [HOW]

       of school age [WHO]

       drinking [WHAT].’

By clearly setting out the aims of your review from the outset, you plan a route for the journey
that you will follow for the rest of the process. For example, a good review question can
inform your literature search, as you will find it easier to identify the key concepts of the
question and therefore your key search terms (we explore this fully in Chapter 5). A clear review
question shapes your inclusion criteria (in helping you to select studies) as you will know
precisely what type of study you are looking for (Stern et al., 2014) – explored within Chapter 6.
  For now, we offer a brief worked example (Box 4.2).

      Example of a review question informing inclusion criteria
      Review question: What are the economic and social implications of school-age drinking?

         Inclusion criteria

         Study must include:

         1. Subjects of school age (5–16 years) [WHO].
         2. Drinking of any alcohol in any quantity [WHAT].
         3. Economic and/or social implications [HOW].

=== p. 97 ===

When you know approximately how many records your searches are likely to retrieve (and perhaps,
by screening a sample, can even estimate how many studies you are actually likely to include) you
can realistically predict how many days your review will take. This helps you to decide whether
you need to enlist the help of colleagues; to obtain items not available through your library or
through electronic collections; and to take account of other factors with associated costs. It is
much better to plan ahead and budget for such costs than to encounter them as a surprise.
  You should also check for existing reviews on the topic. Do not just look at published
reviews – check PROSPERO (https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/prospero/) or other study registers for
protocols of ongoing reviews, to make sure that you are not duplicating effort. If your research
team identifies another group who are planning a review in a closely related topic, you could
contact them with a view to possible collaboration. If you are a student you may decide to move
on to a unique, but related, topic.

   Existing reviews
   Consider a stepwise approach when including evidence in a rapid review. Start by assessing
   how far you can get using existing systematic reviews, either using their results or using any
   studies they have already found. Then move on to higher quality designs for primary studies.
   Try searching specialist databases of reviews or use search filters and publication type limits
   for reviews to identify the highest level evidence first.

Defining your scope with your audience in mind
You need to consider your review audience when defining the scope for your review. Is your
review question the one that your audience most wants answering? To check, you could consult
key people with an interest in, or affected by, your review to confirm that your review question
is appropriate. If your review findings are likely to inform professional practice or to enable
decision-makers to change policy, contact experts for their input.
  Where you identify strong commercial, community or professional interest in your review
findings, you will find it wise to involve key opinion leaders or stakeholders from the outset and
jointly devise the review question (Harris et al., 2016; Greenhalgh et al., 2019). Numerous examples demonstrate how users can contribute to systematic reviews (Bayliss et al., 2016; Pollock
et al., 2018). This does not mean that individuals dictate your review findings but does allow
you to define a review question that is useful for your target audience.

Frameworks for defining your scope
In deciding your scope, you will find it helpful to identify the key concepts using a question
framework. One of the most popular question frameworks is ‘PICOS’ (Population; Intervention;
Comparator; Outcome; Study type).

The PICOS framework
The PICOS framework (Table 4.1) was developed within evidence-based medicine to explore
questions that explore the effectiveness of an intervention, whether related to health or not.

Table 4.1      The elements of PICOS

Population             Who is facing the problem or situation that needs to be addressed? In a
                       human population, for example, which age, sex, socioeconomic or ethnic
                       groups are involved? In which countries?

Intervention           How might the problem or situation be tackled? What interventions might
                       be used and how are those options best described using technical terms, lay
(or
                       terms, synonyms and related terms?
Exposure)
                       If your topic does not relate to an intervention (i.e. a planned response), you
                       may find it helpful to replace Intervention with Exposure (an unintentional
                       occurrence or happening); for example, exposure to air pollution.

Comparison             What is the alternative? You may wish to compare two or more possible
                       options or exposures to explore which is either most likely to have an
                       intended effect or most likely to happen. This facet is optional when your
                       question does not involve comparing options. Remember that comparing
                       a new option to what is currently done (usual practice) or doing nothing is
                       equally valid.
                       So, you may seek information on the relative merits of:

                       •   buses versus trams for urban congestion;
                       •   natural versus chemical methods of agricultural pest control;
                       • surgery versus drug treatments for an illness.

Outcome(s)             Another optional facet – what are you trying to achieve or accomplish
                       (outcomes) and how would you measure or demonstrate that they have
                       been achieved (outcome measures)? You may find these challenging to
                       identify. You should make sure that you do not introduce potential bias
                       to your review by only searching for anticipated or welcome outcomes.
                       Unexpected or negative outcomes can be equally useful in informing
                       decision-making.

Study type(s)          What types of study are likely to explore your question? Are some study
                       types more useful than others because they are more rigorous (hierarchy
                       of evidence) or more realistic (‘pragmatic’)? If certain study types are more
                       useful have your scoping searches demonstrated that sufficient rigorously
                       designed studies exist so that you will not need to include less reliable
                       study types?

all five facets within your search strategy. Your search strategy aims to retrieve the essential facets
(those which – were they absent – would lead you to reject a study) in order to reduce the literature to a manageable size. You can then examine the results you retrieve more closely at the
screening stage, either skimming or searching within them for additional terms. We will focus
more on study selection in Chapter 6.

Other frameworks for defining your scope
Many frameworks were developed to answer specific types of question. As noted above, PICOS (originally PICO: Population–Intervention–Comparison–Outcome(s)), was developed to answer medical
queries (Richardson et al., 1995; Munn et al., 2018b). For qualitative questions, you may prefer
to use SPIDER (Sample–Phenomenon of Interest–Design–Evaluation–Research type – Cooke et al.,
2012) or SPICE (Setting–Perspective–Intervention/Interest–Comparison–Evaluation – Booth, 2004;
Butler et al., 2017). An alternative version of PICO, known as Context–Intervention–Mechanisms–
Outcomes (CIMO) (Box 4.3) may be useful for management or realist synthesis questions (Denyer &
Tranfield, 2009). A comprehensive list of 38 such frameworks is included in a supplement on framing questions for complex interventions (Booth et al., 2019b). Further advice on framing questions
is available in Chapters 8 and 9 on qualitative and mixed methods reviews, respectively.
  However, when conceptual frameworks are translated into practical search strategies they
may not lead to optimal retrieval. They may retrieve too many (sensitivity/recall) or too few
(specificity) results (Frandsen et al., 2020). Not all frameworks are suitable for fully comprehensive reviews (Methley et al., 2014; Frandsen et al., 2020). Often, you may find it sufficient to
search for 2–3 of the most important concepts or facets.

   Context–intervention–mechanisms–outcomes (CIMO) framework

       Context – Which individuals, relationships, institutional settings or wider systems am I
       studying?

       Intervention – For what event, action or activity am I studying the effects? (As with
       PICOS, you may be exploring an ‘exposure’ resulting from a change in circumstances
       rather than a deliberate intervention.)

       Mechanisms – What responses explain how the event, action or activity leads to an
       effect? Under what circumstances are these responses activated or not activated?

       Outcomes – In what effects of the intervention (planned or unintentional) are you interested?
       How are such effects measured?

      ‘Under what conditions (C) does leadership style (I) influence how project teams perform (O),
      and by what mechanisms does leadership style (I) impact on project team performance (O)?’
      (Denyer & Tranfield, 2009)

Complex interventions
The examples given so far are straightforward and involve simple ‘does this work?’ type questions.
Review questions are more difficult to define when they involve interventions which are complex
(Petticrew et al., 2013b) – for example, involving multiple different components or populations.
    When many variables and forces act upon a situation, it is impossible to be certain that an outcome is a direct result of a single intervention or exposure; you may need to adopt a systems-based
approach to fully understand the context (Moore et al., 2019; Petticrew et al., 2019). Complex
interventions can also be investigated using realist methods (involving looking not only at what
works but also for whom, in what circumstances, and why) (Petticrew, 2015) (see Chapter 9).
    Interventions are described as ‘complex’ (Guise et al., 2017) if they have:

•     multiple components (intervention complexity)
•     complicated/multiple causal pathways, feedback loops, synergies and/or mediators and
      moderators of effect (pathway complexity).

In addition, they may also:

•     target multiple participants/groups/organisational levels (population complexity)
•     require multifaceted adoption, uptake or integration strategies (implementation complexity)
•     work in a dynamic multi-dimensional environment (contextual complexity). (Based on
      Guise et al., 2017)

With so much written on how to design and how to evaluate complex interventions, this chapter can only go into brief detail and we recommend that you refer to ‘Suggestions for further
information’ at the end of this chapter.
  Consider the example intervention below (Box 4.4). We have listed just some of the ways in
which it could be considered complex.

   Example of a complex intervention
   Education programmes to teach type 1 diabetic patients how to self-manage their condition
   effectively:

   • Number of interacting components: lecture-type sessions, small-group role play, diary
     keeping, discussion of problems/successes amongst group.
   • Delivery of the intervention: intense week-long course, weekly sessions, online packages.
   • Change of behaviour in participants, enabling them to increase control of their own condition and integrate effective self-management into their daily lives.
   • Change in behaviour in health professionals: less ‘do as I say’, more about patient
     empowerment.
   • Programme can be tailored to an individual: one-to-one sessions available to discuss specific goals and targets.
   • Therapist effect: delivered by diabetic nurses or dietitians or peer volunteers trained in
     delivery of the education programme, personality types.
   • Outcomes: control of their diabetes, long-term health outcomes (such as heart disease),
     patient-rated quality of life, time off work, admissions to hospital, health care resource use.

This example illustrates the diverse review questions that you could ask around this topic.
So how do you begin to define the scope of your review question for this type of complex
intervention? The PerSPE(c)TiF (Perspective-Setting Phenomenon of interest–Environment–
comparison–Timing–Findings) question structure has been suggested to elicit relevant elements
of complex interventions (Booth et al., 2019c).
  Three issues that relate to complexity might guide how you define your review question
(Squires et al., 2013), as follows.

What type of question am I asking?
As with simpler interventions, you should allow your review question to be guided by what you
or your target audience need to know. Discuss your question widely with colleagues or stakeholders to ensure that it addresses their needs (Squires et al., 2013).
  Just because an intervention is complex, it does not mean your review question necessarily
has to adopt a complex perspective or lens (Petticrew et al., 2013a; 2019) – you can choose to
conduct a straightforward effectiveness review looking at one or more specific outcomes, while
acknowledging the complexity of the intervention and its context.

=== p. 102 ===

   Focusing on important outcomes
   If the findings of your rapid review are to influence practice and policy, then you need to
   select outcomes that are relevant to professionals, policy-makers and the public. Although
   you would usually try to explore a wide diversity of outcomes in a full systematic review you
   will need to telescope this list given the time and resource limitations of a rapid review. You
   should involve your stakeholders in prioritising outcomes.

However, you may decide that you need to know more than whether an intervention is effective
or not. Perhaps you want to consider why an intervention works (mechanisms of action); identify factors which impact upon its effectiveness; ask whether the intervention is implementable
in ‘real life’ (see Chapter 9); or explore the theory underpinning the intervention (Petticrew
et al., 2013a; Squires et al., 2013). Let us consider our exemplar complex intervention again and
identify some review questions that we might ask (Box 4.5).

   Questions that you could ask in a complex intervention review
   • Does it work? What is the average effect?
   • For whom does it work?
       �   For the individual with type 1 diabetes?
       �   For particular subgroups within a type 1 diabetic population?
       �   For family members?
       �   For wider society?

   •   How does the intervention work?
   •   Does any theory underpin the education interventions? What is it based on?
   •   What are the barriers and facilitators of education programmes?
   •   What are the mechanisms of action and how do they interact with each other?

What issues are manageable within the scope of the review?
Should I lump or split?
As seen from our complex intervention example, you may not find it feasible to ask all the
pertinent questions in a single review. So, you also need to consider what is manageable within
the time and resources available. It is likely that much of the evidence will be heterogeneous,
explored within many different study types. To determine how broad the scope of your review
should be, you need to decide whether to ‘lump’ or ‘split’ (Squires et al., 2013) your review questions in order to present the evidence.

=== p. 103 ===

  ‘Lumpers’ maintain that, unless two interventions act in very different ways, they should be
included within the same review. Review findings can thus be applied across the widest possible
range of settings. ‘Splitters’, however, only combine studies that they consider very similar in
terms of design, setting or intervention characteristics. Splitters either exclude a large number
of studies or analyse them in multiple separate categories. No single study type or methodological approach can provide all the answers required to model a complex system (Higgins et al.,
2019; Noyes et al., 2019; Petticrew et al., 2019). Therefore, a mixed methods approach combining quantitative and qualitative data may offer the optimal solution (Petticrew et al., 2019)
(see Chapter 9).
  Considering our example again (Box 4.6):

   Lumping or splitting in our example complex intervention review?

   • Could studies looking at an intense five-day education programme be lumped together
     with studies of a programme which delivers five weekly one-day sessions?
   • Could studies of programmes delivered by trained diabetes nurses and dietitians be compared with studies reporting a peer-delivered programme?
   • Could studies reporting the effects of education programmes on patient quality of life be
     compared with other studies investigating family quality of life?

Thinking about your own review, consider which approach best matches your research topic,
remembering to take into account the resource implications (Weir et al., 2012).

How will I specify the complex intervention?
When defining your review question it is important to describe the intervention of interest as precisely as possible so that you know which studies to include in your review. You
will find it helpful to carry out specific scoping searches to investigate how the intervention is described in the literature (unfortunately, you will often find insufficient detail
is included in the studies, especially for complex interventions). Often we use a 12-item
tool named TIDieR (Template for Intervention Description and Replication) to describe
the components of an intervention (Hoffmann et al., 2014) or our own abridged five-item
version, labelled ‘TIDieR-Lite’ (by whom, what, where, to what intensity and how often)
(Chambers et al., 2020).
  One way to overcome a lack of information about the intervention is to focus your scoping
searches on secondary study types such as process evaluations, which may provide more
detail, or to contact authors directly for further information.

when defining your scope, we suggest several tools to assist with the scoping process, the first
being logic models.

Logic models
A logic model (also known as an impact model or theory of change model) is a type of concept
map, originating from programme evaluation. Logic models are widely used in public health
as summary diagrams of complex interventions, identifying the key components and relationships between inputs, outputs and outcomes (Anderson et al., 2011; Baxter et al., 2014; Rehfuess
  You can use logic models like the one in Figure 4.1 to clarify and prioritise your review questions when defining your scope. They will help you to understand the complexity of the intervention and ensure that you will explore all contributing elements (Squires et al., 2013).

FURTHER DEFINING YOUR SCOPE
Once you have broken down your review question into concepts, you need to consider what
type of review you will undertake; some review methodologies are a better fit than others for
certain types of question.
  It is not always realistic to conduct a full ‘Systematic Review’ but you may choose to systema-tise some stages of the process where resources allow and where it strengthens the credibility
of your review to do so. (For example, when mapping the literature around your topic, you
will want to show that you have conducted a wide-ranging and thorough literature search, but
you may choose not to spend time on formal quality assessment of the studies you identify.)
(See Chapter 6.)
  An ever-expanding literature has labelled different types of review, categorising them by
purpose or methodology (often against the benchmark of the conventional systematic review)
(Munn et al., 2018b; Tricco et al., 2018b). A recent typology of review ‘families’ (traditional
reviews, rapid reviews, qualitative evidence syntheses, etc.) (Sutton et al., 2019) offers a helpful
primer. Meanwhile, Chapter 3 of this book offers guidance on choosing a review method appropriate to your topic.

    The methodology you choose is also influenced by how much literature exists on your topic.
Typically, you refine and focus your research question through a scoping search. Scoping
searches are useful ‘to attain a preliminary assessment of the size and scope of research literature
and to help assess the feasibility of conducting research, including determining whether …
questions have previously been evaluated, or are up-to-date, and for estimating time frames
and budgetary considerations’ (Rathbone et al., 2017). You may start with a relatively clear idea
of the topic that you want to research, and then find that someone else has already comprehensively reviewed it! Be sure to consider the advice we provide in Chapter 2 on the option of
conducting a review update. Alternatively, you might have a fresh idea for a new topic for your
review but find insufficient primary research available to answer your review question.
    By carrying out initial searches of bibliographic databases you can identify where
the research gaps lie and where a new review is needed. Chapter 5 details how to conduct a
scoping search.
    At this stage, once you have some results from a scoping search, you can use other mapping
techniques to further define the scope of your review question. We have already discussed logic
models, which although time consuming and resource intensive are nevertheless useful, particularly for complex reviews. Other mapping processes such as descriptive mapping, data
mining or text mining and concept mapping help to make sense of the quantity and
characteristics of the literature. In this section we outline these tools with examples and links to
resources, where available, that you could use to scope or to map the literature.

Descriptive mapping
Descriptive mapping is used to define a body of literature on a given topic, by allocating keywords to the studies found. Classifying the literature in this way can help to determine what
research has been previously carried out as well as to identify research gaps. Organisations such
as the EPPI-Centre have devised purpose-specific sets of keywords (EPPI-Centre, 2003) but you
may prefer to devise your own keywords tailored to the purposes of your own review.
    Having assigned keywords describing the main intervention for each included study, you
present your results as a descriptive map or table.
    We will revisit the exemplar systematic mapping review below (Table 4.3) several times
throughout this book. The review aimed to identify and map the effectiveness of interventions
to promote physical activity with children in specialist schools (Emonson et al., 2019). The
authors categorised interventions by:

•     type (e.g. dance/drama, games/play, motor activities, running, stretching, swimming and
      ‘other’)
•     duration, where and by whom they are delivered, and
•     outcomes measured (e.g. academic, behavioural, cognitive, physical, psychological, social
      or ‘other’).

Free text fields added detail where required (e.g. a brief description of the interventions). As
this was a mapping review, the map becomes an output in its own right – but for whatever
type of review you are planning, descriptive mapping can help you to finalise the criteria that
locate certain studies within or outside your review scope.

Data mining and automation
Data mining refers to analysing data to identify patterns and correlations. When the data
being analysed is textual, it is referred to as text mining. Both terms are closely linked with
the broader concept of artificial intelligence.
    Data mining takes place behind many websites that we use each day; examples include per-sonalised Google results based on your search history, or algorithms defining which advertisements or posts you see on social media sites.
    In the above examples, the data (on our behaviour) is collected by third parties and we have little control over how it is used; however, we can actively employ similar technology in the review
process.
    For example, when defining your scope you may find that your scoping search returns large
numbers of citations. We look at study selection in more depth in Chapter 6 but several software
tools rank results according to likely relevance (Waffenschmidt et al., 2018; O’Mara-Eves et al.,
2015). Result sets may be generated by text mining (recognising patterns in the results) (Thomas
et al., 2011; Shemilt et al., 2014) and/or machine learning (recognising patterns in decisions made by a user). Once the software has ‘learned’ from sufficient examples, it predicts how
many more results are worth screening manually. Tools include Rayyan (Ouzzani et al., 2016),
Abstrackr and EPPI-Reviewer Web (Tsou et al., 2020).
    An excellent White Paper summarises the applications of text mining within systematic
reviews (Glanville & Wood, 2018).

Concept mapping
Concept mapping (sometimes known as mind mapping or occasionally idea webbing)
involves visualising your research topic and observing the connections between its different
aspects. You may find it helpful when defining your scope, especially if you are visually minded.
    Benefits from using concept maps when planning a literature review include (Rowley & Slack, 2004):

•     identifying key concepts on a research topic;
•     identifying additional search terms;
•     clarifying thinking about the structure of the literature review;
•     understanding theory, concepts and the relationships between them.

Concept analysis
If your preliminary sensitisation to the literature in your topic reveals a lack of clarity around
one or more key concepts, you could undertake a concept analysis to gain a better understanding. Concept analysis has been used in several disciplines but is best established in the field of
nursing. Our guidance is based on the model developed by Walker and Avant (2010). Concept
analysis seeks to define and clarify a concept through examples of its usage in the available
literature, and works well for wide-ranging questions and terminology which may be used in
a variety of ways. Concept analysis involves naming a concept of interest (and the purpose for
which analysis is being conducted), before going on to identify all the situations in which it
may be used – its defining attributes and its ‘surrogate terms’. It establishes its antecedents and
consequences (the context before and after it is used) and constructs a ‘model case’, before looking for

DEFINING YOUR SCOPE: CHALLENGES AND PITFALLS
As with any stage of a literature review, certain challenges and pitfalls occur when defining your
scope. These range from minor ‘nuisance’ complications, such as identifying duplicate and/or
multiple citations, to substantive challenges such as deciding which types of studies to include
or exclude and how to assess their quality (Kennedy, 2007). In this section, we identify common
problems when defining the scope for your own projects.

How wide do I go? (Sampling approaches)
A common challenge when defining your scope relates to knowing ‘when to stop’. Debate cen-tres on whether you should aim for comprehensiveness (in quantitative terms), theoretical
saturation (in qualitative terms) or a ‘representative’ approach. Alternatively, you might aim
to cover key ‘pivotal’ works in your chosen topic (as for a meta-narrative review or a concept
analysis). Whichever approach you choose, and your choice may be influenced by the time
and resources available, you should be explicit in your methods so that the reader is fully aware
of the scope of your review, and the criteria for selection and relevance (Bruce, 2001).
  The type of review you choose will help you to define your scope, as reviews differ in the
extent to which they use and report systematic methods. Table 4.5 outlines three review types
(scoping, mapping, systematic) and their respective purposes. For each review type the table

How narrow can I stay? (Scope creep)
A major problem in the literature review process is scope creep: uncontrolled changes over
time to your review aims, direction, scale and/or timescale and, more specifically, the gradual
unchecked expansion of the review objectives. Scope creep can occur at any point during the
review process, particularly when you are searching the literature. You should constantly refer to
your original scope to ensure that your project does not go off track. A common tension exists
between what is interesting and what is useful – references may catch your attention, but may
not be directly relevant to your research question. Of course, you may face having to amend your
original scope. You can manage this using a changes clause (Matchar et al., 2006), which is particularly important when working on official contracts and/or as part of a wider research team.

THE REVIEW PROTOCOL
Once you have defined your review scope, you can start writing the review protocol. As with any
research project, you need to plan your literature review carefully (see Chapter 2). Your protocol
should lay out in advance how you will approach each step of your review process.
     The review protocol has three main functions:

1.     Protection against bias – by clearly describing your methods you are ensuring rigour and
       minimising the risk of being subjective or otherwise inconsistent in your judgement.
2.     Setting the rules for the conduct of the review – this is particularly useful when working
       within a wider project team. Agreeing a protocol ensures that everyone follows the same procedure. If everyone sticks to what they said they would do, the protocol, with tense changed from
       the future to past tense, becomes the first time-saving contribution to your final report!
3.     Staking your claim to the topic – this is particularly important if you plan to publish your review.

When review authors publish in advance a review protocol that they have written prior to examining in detail the available studies, this:

      reduces the impact of review authors’ biases, promotes transparency of methods and processes, reduces the potential for duplication, allows peer review of the planned methods before
      they have been completed, and offers an opportunity for the review team to plan resources
      and logistics for undertaking the review itself. (Lasserson et al., 2021)

SUMMARY
By defining your review scope you provide a solid foundation for your subsequent review.
You may find it useful to use a published framework for formulating your research question.
Conducting a scoping search can help you to assess if your review is feasible, both in terms of
uniqueness (are there existing reviews in your topic?) and in terms of the size and characteristics
of the evidence base.
  Consider mapping the literature identified in your scoping search. Various tools and
approaches can assist with this, including logic models, data mining and concept mapping.

=== p. 121 ===

  Consider whether you aim to be comprehensive or selective – this will be informed by the
literature and the type of review that you choose to undertake.
  Beware of scope creep, where items of interest distract from the original aim of your review.
You can avoid scope creep by drawing up a review protocol at the outset and following it during
your decision-making, particularly when you implement inclusion and exclusion criteria. If you
need to change the scope of your review you can manage this by including a changes clause
in your protocol.

   • Formulate your research question using a published framework (such as PICOS) where
     possible.
   • When defining the scope of your review think carefully about what your audience will
     expect and need.
   • Conduct a scoping search on key databases to identify the literature available.
   • Consider mapping the literature using a tool such as a logic model or similar.
   • Beware of the challenges and pitfalls, such as scope creep.
   • Complete a review protocol with planned milestones before you start on your review.

                     frequently asked questions (FAQs)

   FAQ 4.1 None of the frameworks fit my question. How can I formulate my
   research idea?
   Not every question suits the frameworks covered here. Even if your question does fit a framework, you may choose to omit one or more facets. The important thing is to break down
   your research question into discrete concepts which you can clearly define and translate into
   meaningful search terms.

   FAQ 4.2 With so much literature in my review topic, how can I review it
   systematically?
   If you find more literature than you can realistically review, you can either narrow your focus
   (e.g. look at a more specific type of intervention, or population) or place restrictions on the
   type of evidence you will include. For example, you may apply limits by date, by study type, geographical coverage, or similar. Make sure that you clearly record all your decisions and that you
   are aware of potential biases. Consider the time taken for each stage of the process (not just
   screening but also quality assessment and data extraction) to decide what is feasible. Consider
   whether text mining or data mining might assist you with the study screening process.

   FAQ 4.3 My research question is actually four or five questions, is this a
   problem?
   Given the variety of possible questions that you might like to ask when defining your review
   scope you need to limit these to what is manageable and answerable. You will otherwise

=== p. 122 ===

   discover the hard way that it is not possible to answer all the questions. Consider using a
   logic model to prioritise your research question. Time spent planning at the beginning of your
   review is time well spent to avoid tackling an unanswerable question. You may also want to
   explore Chapter 9 on using a mixed methods review to answer related quantitative and qualitative questions (e.g. the effectiveness, acceptability and implementation of intervention X
   compared with intervention Y).

   FAQ 4.4 I had identified a defined question but my scoping search identified
   nothing on this topic, what shall I do?
   You may need to modify your topic by expanding one or more of your concepts (do this one
   step at a time, so you can assess the impact of expanding each concept), or consider relaxing your inclusion criteria. Defining your research question is iterative. It is not uncommon to
   have to adjust your scope. It is better to adjust your scope early on than to get too far into the
   review process before realising that your question is not answerable.

   FAQ 4.5 Is it really necessary to formally complete the review protocol?
   Yes, your review will benefit greatly from having a formal review protocol. It serves as a document to which you can constantly refer to keep your review on track. The review protocol is
   particularly useful when working within a larger review team as it ensures that you all follow
   the same review recipe. In addition, a well-written review protocol assists when writing the
   methodology section of your review – if you have achieved what you said you would do, you
   should simply be able to change the future tense into the past tense.

   FAQ 4.6 When is it appropriate to change my review scope?
   You may have multiple valid reasons for changing the scope during your review. Spending
   time planning and scoping before you start reduces the risk of having to change, but sometimes change is inevitable due to resourcing, funding or scheduling issues. By conducting a
   scoping search you should become familiar with the literature, reducing the likelihood that
   you will need to change direction further down the line. You should also ensure that no recent
   review exists on your topic (remember to check PROSPERO, and other systematic review
   registers, for similar reviews which are already underway).
