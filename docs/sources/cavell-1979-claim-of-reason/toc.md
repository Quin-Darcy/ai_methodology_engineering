## Preface to new paperback edition (excerpt — what the book is)

Cavell's 1999 Preface explicitly addresses the suggestion that *The Claim of Reason* "is really two books":

> Parts One through Three form, let us suppose, an academically recognizable study of the creation and conflict of skepticism and the ordinary by and with each other — skepticism as understood in Descartes and Hume and Kant, and the ordinary in language as articulated in the practice of J. L. Austin and the thought of the later Wittgenstein — together with implications of this study of knowledge (or say of our responsibility for our speech) for moral encounter. Part Four, by far the longest single part, then takes an unpredicted leap from, or against, the relative consecutiveness of the earlier parts, setting forth a sequence of variously discontinuous responses to that earlier material, questioning and extending its questions, encouraging a freedom of responsiveness to the fact that philosophy continuously finds itself averse to various ordinary words it seems unable to do without.

Two terms the Preface signals as load-bearing for the book's stance:

- **Acknowledgment** vs. **Avoidance** — the fundamental contrast Part Four explores. Skepticism about other minds is recast not as an epistemic failure but as an ethical-existential refusal: the skeptic *avoids* what could otherwise be acknowledged. The "claim" of reason in the title is the demand that another's existence makes on me; reason's job is not to satisfy that claim by proof but to track how the claim is acknowledged or avoided.
- **Criteria** (in Wittgenstein's sense, as Cavell reads them) — not necessary-and-sufficient conditions but the public grammar by which we know what counts as something, where "knowing" remains finite and revisable. Parts One–Three develop this sense of criteria; Part Four extends it to the ethical case.

## Operational map for the exploratory-default deliverable

Per the manifest, **Part IV ("Skepticism and the Problem of Others: Between Acknowledgment and Avoidance", pp. 329–496)** is the operational core. It is the longest part of the book and the one Cavell himself flags as taking "an unpredicted leap" — its discontinuous, "road-sign"-organized form is itself part of its philosophical content.

Within Part IV, the most directly operative sub-sections for an exploratory-default disposition:

- **"Between Acknowledgment and Avoidance" (pp. 329–354 area)** — the framing distinction. Skepticism construed as the temptation to substitute knowledge-claims for the more difficult work of acknowledgment. The exploratory disposition is precisely the discipline of *not* avoiding what is asked of one in conversation.
- **"Toward others we live our skepticism" (p. 437)** — Cavell's terse statement of the existential thesis: skepticism about others is not held as a position but enacted in how one stands toward them.
- **"Asymmetries between the two directions of skepticism" (p. 451)** and **"Passiveness and activeness; the Friend and the Confessor" (p. 459)** — relational vocabulary directly transferrable to a CLAUDE.md disposition that distinguishes charitable engagement from forensic refutation.
- **"Confinement and exposure in knowing" (p. 432)** — the affective texture of trying to know another, useful for grounding the disposition's stance toward intellectual exposure.

For directive extraction, Part IV alone is the candidate scope. Parts One–Three are useful as the conceptual ground (criteria, the ordinary, the inheritance of Wittgenstein and Austin) but are too long and too technical to extract directives from in a v1 pass. Within Part IV, the natural chunking granularity is two-to-five contiguous road-sign sections per chunk; the parts cohere thematically rather than by formal heading-structure.
