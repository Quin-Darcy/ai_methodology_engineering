## Translator's Note (excerpt — methodological orientation)

The Translator's Note (Michael Chase) makes one methodological point that bears on how the book should be read:

> The first stage of Hadot's method is a scrupulous, textually critical reading of the original texts, followed by an equally exacting translation of these texts into French. Only on the foundation of the intense, detailed confrontation with the text which real translation demands, Hadot feels, can one begin the processes of exegesis, interpretation, and, perhaps, criticism. Thus, Hadot's thought is, at least to a large extent, based on his methods of translation.

For this reason, Chase notes that he has translated Hadot's French translations of Greek/Latin/German rather than substituting standard English translations of the underlying ancient texts — the result diverges from familiar English Plato/Plotinus/Marcus, but it preserves Hadot's interpretive position.

## Editor's Introduction (excerpt — what Hadot's project is)

Davidson's Introduction frames the book's central claim: that Greco-Roman philosophy was a *way of life*, not primarily a *body of doctrine*. The "spiritual exercises" of the title are practices — of attention, of dialogue, of memorization, of the *vue d'en haut* (view from above) — by which a philosophical life is shaped, distinct from the textual production by which philosophy is now read. The volume's core polemical move is to recover this practical-formative dimension, which the modern academic conception of philosophy has occluded. Spiritual exercises are accordingly *not* religious in the post-Christian sense; the term "spiritual" is meant to mark that they engage the whole person (intellect, imagination, affect, body) rather than only the propositional intellect.

## Operational map for the exploratory-default deliverable

Per the manifest, the operational targets are:

- **Ch. 1 ("Forms of Life and Forms of Discourse in Ancient Philosophy", pp. 49–70)** — the methodological core; argues that ancient philosophical *texts* were composed *for* particular forms of life, so reading them as decontextualized doctrinal arguments fundamentally misreads them. Directly bears on the exploratory disposition's stance toward classical sources: not as positions to be analyzed, but as records of practices to be reanimated.
- **Ch. 3 ("Spiritual Exercises", pp. 81–125)** — the canonical statement of what spiritual exercises are: catalog and analysis of attention (*prosochē*), meditation, dialogue, the view from above, the contemplation of death, etc. The longest single chapter and the operational backbone for any extraction of practice-level directives.

Beyond these two, several others contribute to the exploratory disposition:

- **Ch. 5 ("The Figure of Socrates", pp. 147–178)** — Socratic dialogue construed as a spiritual exercise (the elenchus as care of the self, not refutation); pairs naturally with the disposition's stance on dialogical philosophy.
- **Ch. 9 ("The View from Above", pp. 238–250)** — short, concentrated essay on the cosmic-perspectival exercise; useful for grounding the disposition's recommendation to step outside one's own framing of a question.
- **Ch. 10 ("The Sage and the World", pp. 251–263)** — the mature stance the practices aim at; useful as the formal endpoint the practices serve.
- **Ch. 11 ("Philosophy as a Way of Life", pp. 264–276)** — the title essay; the most general statement of the position.
- **Postscript: Interview with Pierre Hadot (pp. 277–286)** — late, retrospective; Hadot's own framing of how the project relates to contemporary practice and to Foucault's reading of him.

For directive extraction, **Chs. 1, 3, 11** are the strongest candidates, with Chs. 5 and 9 as secondary. Chunking by chapter is natural; Ch. 3 will likely benefit from sub-chunking by exercise.
