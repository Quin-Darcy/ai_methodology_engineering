## Preface (excerpt — what the volume is)

The editors describe the volume as "a carefully curated tour of a range of meta-philosophical perspectives" rather than an attempt to impose a single conception of methodology. About a third of the volume (Part IV) is given to philosophy's connections with other disciplines, reflecting the editors' view that "philosophy draws on, contributes to, and connects fields outside philosophy" (p. v).

The volume's stated limitation: the entries "all concern what we can coarsely describe as the 'Western tradition' of philosophy, and are largely Anglo-American in approach. The volume is already massive (pushing the limits of what the publisher would accept), and adding a correspondingly full range of entries on other approaches was out of the question" (p. vi). For non-Western methodological self-reflection (Indian, Chinese, Islamic, African philosophical traditions), this volume should be supplemented.

The four-part structure: Part I asks the meta-question "What is philosophical methodology?"; Part II surveys traditions chronologically; Part III treats specific methodological topics; Part IV explores philosophy's interfaces with neighbouring disciplines.

## Operational map for the research-procedure deliverable

This Handbook is **the single largest gap** Report 1 identified in the prior baseline. For Tier 1 use in the research-procedure document, the most operative chapters depend on the procedure's coverage:

**Methodological-tradition chapters (Part II)** — directly relevant when the procedure document needs to identify which methodological tradition a question lives in:
- **Ch. 2 (Normore, "The Methodology of the History of Philosophy", p. 27)** — historiographical methodology; pairs with Skinner / Rorty.
- **Ch. 3 (Soames, "19th/20th C. Analytic Philosophy Methodology", p. 49)** — the analytic tradition's methodological self-understanding.
- **Ch. 6 (Baz, "Ordinary Language Philosophy", p. 112)** — for ordinary-language-tradition questions.
- **Ch. 8 (Kornblith, "Philosophical Naturalism", p. 147)** — for naturalistic-empiricist procedures (relevant to the procedure document's positioning vs. systematic-review methodology).
- **Ch. 10 (Carman, "Phenomenology", p. 179)** — for continental-tradition questions.
- **Ch. 11 (Jackman, "The Pragmatic Method", p. 193)** — pragmatist methodology.

**Methodology-topic chapters (Part III)** — directly relevant for specific procedural moves:
- **Ch. 12 (Cath, "Reflective Equilibrium", p. 213)** — the iterative-balance methodology Rawls codified; relevant for the procedure document's synthesis stage.
- **Ch. 14 (King, "Philosophical and Conceptual Analysis", p. 249)** — what conceptual analysis is doing methodologically.
- **Ch. 16 (Weinberg, "Intuitions", p. 287)** — connects to Mode 4 (Dennettian critic) and exploratory.
- **Ch. 17 (Gutting, "Philosophical Progress", p. 309)** — what counts as progress in philosophy; directly bears on what a research report aims to deliver.
- **Ch. 19 (Hájek, "Philosophical Heuristics and Methodology", p. 348)** — applied heuristics for doing philosophical work; relevant for the procedure's operational guidance.
- **Ch. 20 (Kelly, "Disagreement in Philosophy", p. 374)** — what to do with persistent disagreement; relevant for the procedure's handling of genuinely contested forks.

**Interface chapters (Part IV)** — relevant for cross-disciplinary questions:
- **Ch. 26 (Beiser, "History of Ideas: A Defense", p. 505)** — Skinner-friendly historiographical position; pairs with Mode 3.
- **Ch. 27 (List & Valentini, "The Methodology of Political Theory", p. 525)** — political-philosophy methodology specifically.
- **Ch. 35 (Maitra, "Feminism", p. 690)** — feminist methodology, including critique of charity demands.

For an initial extraction pass building the research procedure, **Chs. 1, 2, 12, 14, 17, 19, 20, 26, 27** are the strongest candidates. The volume is too large for a single extraction pass; chunking by chapter (one chunk per file, named by chapter number) is the natural granularity.
