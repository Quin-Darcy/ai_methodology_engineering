## The four genres (the conceptual map)

The title "Four Genres" refers to four kinds of philosophical historiography that Rorty distinguishes throughout the essay; the genres are not in 1:1 correspondence with the section numbers above. The four genres are:

1. **Rational reconstruction** — treating great dead philosophers as contemporaries; reconstructing their arguments in current philosophical terms. Discussed in §I.
2. **Historical reconstruction** — recovering what the philosopher would have said to their actual contemporaries, in their own terms; subject to the Skinnerian constraint (no agent can be said to have meant something they could not have accepted as a description of what they meant). Discussed in §I.
3. **Geistesgeschichte** — the canon-forming, large-scale narrative that explains why the questions we now ask came to seem important. Discussed in §II.
4. **Doxography** — the genre Rorty argues should "wither away": the chronological listing of philosophical positions on a fixed canonical question set, treated as ahistorical. Discussed in §II as the foil to Geistesgeschichte.

Rorty's positive proposal (§IV): the first three genres are "indispensable and do not compete with one another"; intellectual history is the broader genre within which all of them have a legitimate place. The dilemma between rational and historical reconstruction is false — both should be done, but separately and self-consciously.

## Methodological framing (excerpt from §I)

> Analytic philosophers who have attempted 'rational reconstructions' of the arguments of great dead philosophers have done so in the hope of treating these philosophers as contemporaries, as colleagues with whom they can exchange views… It is urged that we should not force Aristotle or Kant to take sides in current debates within philosophy of language or metaethics. There seems to be a dilemma: either we anachronistically impose enough of our problems and vocabulary on the dead to make them conversational partners, or we confine our interpretive activity to making their falsehoods look less silly by placing them in the context of the benighted times in which they were written. Those alternatives, however, do not constitute a dilemma. We should do both of these things, but do them separately. (p. 49)

This is the canonical pluralist counterpoint to Skinner's contextualism — and the central methodological move the essay makes available to a research procedure that engages historical figures.
