## Introduction (excerpt — methodological framing on the principle of charity)

Davidson states the unifying question of the essays: "What is it for words to mean what they do? In the essays collected here I explore the idea that we would have an answer to this question if we knew how to construct a theory satisfying two demands: it would provide an interpretation of all utterances, actual and potential, of a speaker or group of speakers; and it would be verifiable without knowledge of the detailed propositional attitudes of the speaker" (Introduction, p. xiii).

The principle of charity is stated explicitly in the Introduction (p. xvii): the devices for separating belief and meaning "all of them, in one way or another, rely on the Principle of Charity." Davidson traces the phrase to Neil Wilson, "Substances Without Substrata," and to Quine's *Word and Object* (p. 59). His own application: "I apply the Principle of Charity across the board. So applied, it counsels us quite generally to prefer theories of interpretation that minimize disagreement… The aim of interpretation is not agreement but understanding. My point has always been that understanding can be secured only by interpreting in a way that makes for the right sort of agreement."

For the **research procedure** (target use): Essay 9 ("Radical Interpretation") and Essay 13 ("On the Very Idea of a Conceptual Scheme") are the canonical primary sources cited across the literature on the principle of charity. Essays 10–12 (the rest of the Radical Interpretation section) develop the position; the Introduction itself contains the most concise statement of the principle.

For the **exploratory mode** (target use): the same Essay 9 plus Essay 13, supplemented by the Introduction's discussion of charity as constitutive of interpretation rather than as a moral norm.
