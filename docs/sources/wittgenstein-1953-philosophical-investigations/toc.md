## Editors' Note (verbatim — significant for how Part II is treated)

> What appears as Part I of this volume was complete by 1945. Part II was written between 1946 and 1949. If Wittgenstein had published his work himself, he would have suppressed a good deal of what is in the last thirty pages or so of Part I and worked what is in Part II, with further material, into its place. We have had to decide between variant readings for words and phrases throughout the manuscript. The choice never affected the sense.

This editorial framing is itself the argument of the 4th-edition revision (which renames Part II as "Philosophy of Psychology — A Fragment" precisely to register that it was not finished material).

## Contents — Part I — pp. 1–197 (printed pages)

Part I has no chapter divisions. It is a sequence of numbered remarks §§ 1 – 693, occasionally grouped by larger thematic blocks signaled in the text itself rather than by formal headings. The conventional citation form is "PI §nnn".

Notable internal landmarks (commonly cited as cohesive sub-sequences):

| Range | Conventional name | Brief description |
|---|---|---|
| §§ 1–88 | Augustinian picture; meaning as use; family resemblance | Sets up the diagnostic targets (Augustine on language, the *Tractatus* picture theory) and the alternative tools (language-games, family resemblance, criteria). |
| **§§ 89–133** | **The methodological remarks** ★ | **The most concentrated statement of philosophical method in PI: philosophy as descriptive, not theoretical; "philosophy leaves everything as it is" (§124); "the work of the philosopher consists in assembling reminders for a particular purpose" (§127); "the philosopher's treatment of a question is like the treatment of an illness" (§255 echoes); the method's rejection of theses (§128).** |
| §§ 134–242 | Rule-following | Truth and proposition; rules and the regress argument; "obeying a rule is a practice" (§202); the rule-following considerations Kripke later read as central. |
| §§ 243–315 | Private-language argument | Whether a private language is possible; the beetle-in-the-box (§293); criteria for inner states. |
| §§ 316–428 | Thought, intention, meaning | What thinking is; the temptation to picture thought as a mental process. |
| §§ 429–570 | Understanding, intending, expecting | "Now I understand" — what is added when one understands? |
| §§ 571–693 | Intentionality, picturing, meaning-as-use revisited | Imagination, expectation, hope, intention; closing remarks return to the diagnostic frame of the opening. |

## Operational map for the exploratory-default deliverable

Per the manifest, **§§ 89–133 of Part I** is the operational core for the exploratory-default disposition. These remarks are the most concentrated statement Wittgenstein gives of what philosophy is *doing* (or should be doing) — and they map almost directly onto the disposition the slim-core CLAUDE.md is meant to enact:

- §89: philosophy is concerned with "the order of our knowledge", but the order it gives is one we always already had, only obscured.
- §109: "Philosophy is a battle against the bewitchment of our intelligence by means of language."
- §116: "What we do is to bring words back from their metaphysical to their everyday use."
- §122: "A main source of our failure to understand is that we do not *command a clear view* of the use of our words. — Our grammar is lacking in this sort of perspicuity. A perspicuous representation produces just that understanding which consists in 'seeing connections.'"
- §123: "A philosophical problem has the form: 'I don't know my way about.'"
- §124: "Philosophy may in no way interfere with the actual use of language; it can in the end only describe it. ... It leaves everything as it is."
- §127: "The work of the philosopher consists in assembling reminders for a particular purpose."
- §128: "If one tried to advance theses in philosophy, it would never be possible to debate them, because everyone would agree to them."
- §133: the closure: clarity, philosophy as activity, "the real discovery is the one which makes me capable of stopping doing philosophy when I want to."

These remarks justify the exploratory disposition's *style* (descriptive, non-theoretical, oriented toward perspicuous representation rather than thesis-defense) and license the rejection of forced systematicity and pseudo-precision.

For directive extraction, **§§89–133 are the primary candidates**, with the surrounding §§ 65–88 (family resemblance, language-games) as the conceptual ground that must be in scope to read 89–133 properly. Part II §xi (aspect-seeing) is a useful secondary target for the disposition's stance toward "seeing-as" — i.e., the way philosophical understanding is more often a shift in what one notices than a discovery of a new fact.

For chunking, **§§89–133 should be a single chunk** (`chunks/01-pi-1-89-133-method.pdf`), with optional secondary chunks for §§65–88 (family resemblance) and Part II §xi (aspect-seeing). The book is too long and stylistically too dense to extract from in a single sweep; the targeted §§89–133 chunk is the v1 path.
