## Preface (excerpt — what the book is doing)

Palmer's stated purpose is to give a "fundamental clarification of the development, meaning, and scope of hermeneutics itself" as a prerequisite for understanding its implications for literary interpretation (p. xiii). The book treats hermeneutics as "the field" rather than as a single doctrine; the use of "hermeneutics" with the *s* (vs. "hermeneutic") preserves room to refer to the field generically and to specific theorists' positions individually.

The book's structure mirrors a methodological argument: Part I defines the field and surveys its definitional disputes; Part II reconstructs the four theorists chronologically (Schleiermacher → Dilthey → Heidegger → Gadamer) to show how the discipline develops; Part III ("A Hermeneutical Manifesto") translates the inherited theory into directly usable theses for the American literary-interpretation context.

For a Mode 2 (hermeneutic close-reading) skill, **Part III is the operationally most directly usable material** — Chapter 14's "Thirty Theses on Interpretation" is a self-contained list of moves that can be lifted as candidate directives. Chapter 13 sets up the theses by re-asking "what is interpretation?" in terms inherited from Part II.

For breadth and history of the field, Part II's theorist chapters (esp. Chs. 11–12 on Gadamer) are the canonical English-language pedagogical introduction.
